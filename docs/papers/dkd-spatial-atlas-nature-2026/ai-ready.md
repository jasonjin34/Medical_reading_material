## Abstract
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), the leading cause of kidney failure, is marked by clinical and molecular heterogeneity, making therapeutic development exceedingly difficult1. Here we used Xenium and CosMx single-cell spatial transcriptomics, integrated with single-nucleus RNA sequencing, to build a cross-platform kidney atlas that makes tissue architecture computable for prognosis, non-invasive detection and patient selection. Using this atlas, we defined reproducible tissue niches and injury-linked microenvironments and uncovered a profibrotic context that expands with disease and tracks with worse kidney function. Within this architecture, we identified a B cell-predominant, tertiary lymphoid structure-like immune microenvironment that defines a distinct DKD subset with accelerated progression to renal end-points. We developed tissue biomarkers and a matched plasma protein panel that capture this biology, stratify patients in a population biobank and improve risk prediction beyond clinical models—supporting their potential for biomarker-guided selection in future B cell-targeted DKD trials.

### Main
The human kidney is a highly complex organ whose three-dimensional architecture enables essential functions such as fluid and electrolyte balance, waste excretion and blood pressure regulation2–4. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by a persistent reduction in glomerular filtration rate (GFR), accumulation of toxins, salt and water retention, and abnormalities in electrolyte and hormonal balance5. If untreated, kidney disease can lead to end-stage renal failure, requiring life-sustaining dialysis or transplantation. DKD is the leading cause of CKD and develops in approximately 40% of patients with diabetes6,7. Recent therapeutic advances—including sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and selective endothelin receptor antagonists—have demonstrated efficacy in slowing disease progression8–12. Despite these advances, end-stage renal failure remains common, reflecting the heterogeneity of DKD, where disease progression ranges from rapid decline to long-term stability1. With the increasing global burden of diabetes, there is an urgent need for precise, mechanism-based approaches to stratify and treat DKD. Clinical factors such as age, sex, estimated GFR (eGFR) and albuminuria predict outcomes but reveal little about the underlying biology, and genetic risk scores offer limited value for risk or treatment guidance13,14. Circulating tumour necrosis factor (TNF) receptors 1 and 2 strongly associate with renal decline and end-stage renal failure15,16, yet the cellular and spatial context of kidney inflammation in DKD remains unclear, limiting translation into targeted therapies. Next-generation single-cell spatial transcriptomics enables near-genome-wide gene profiling at true cellular resolution within intact tissue, preserving spatial context and revealing disease-relevant tissue organization and distinct pathological subtypes directly from clinical biopsies.

### A spatial atlas at cellular resolution
We generated a single-cell spatial atlas from 64 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) kidney samples from 58 patients (26% female, with a mean age of 57.3 years) spanning the full range of GFR and diverse demographics (Fig. 1b–e and Supplementary Table 1). Samples were profiled using CosMx (1,000 genes, n = 48) and Xenium (5,100 genes, n = 16) platforms, with 656 overlapping transcripts. After filtering low-quality cells, we integrated each spatial dataset with a reference single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) atlas from 150 patients17 (Supplementary Table 2) using scVI18 and scANVI19. Integration performance assessed by the single-cell integration benchmark20 showed that scANVI performed best (Extended Data Fig. 1b). We retained 3,386,822 CosMx cells and 951,041 Xenium cells after filtering and integrated them with scANVI19 into a unified spatial atlas of more than 5 million cells from more than 200 patients (Fig. 1f–i and Extended Data Fig. 2; quality control metrics are shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–8). Unbiased Leiden clustering followed by marker-based annotation identified 20 cell types, all present across samples except 3 that lacked glomeruli (Fig. 1f, Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). The integrated spatial dataset revealed distinct endothelial subtypes, including glomerular, peritubular and descending vasa recta cells, and the higher resolution of Xenium enabled detection of lymphatic endothelium (Fig. 1f). Stromal cells are difficult to distinguish owing to shared markers and spatial overlap, but we identified fibroblasts, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and mesangial cells based on gene expression and location. Mesangial cells showed higher IGFBP5 (CosMx), PDGFRB and IL1RL1 (Xenium) expression (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). Spatial resolution also refined annotation of parietal epithelial cells and the loop of Henle (LOH), distinguishing thick ascending (TAL) from thin descending (DTL) and ascending (ATL) segments (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). We identified injured proximal tubule (iPT), proximal tubule (PT), injured thick ascending limb (iTAL) and thick ascending limb (TAL) cells by injury-associated gene signatures. Differential expression between injured and healthy segments revealed conserved markers across platforms, including SOX4, VCAM1 and HAVCR1 in iPT cells, and CLU, SLPI and ITGB6 in iTAL cells (Extended Data Fig. 2a–d). Because CosMx and Xenium are not genome wide, we imputed 3,000 highly variable genes from the snRNA-seq reference using the scANVI latent space. Imputed expression patterns aligned with known cell identities, as canonical markers localized to expected populations (Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 5). We combined Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)-stained CosMx and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained Xenium data for paired histological and spatial analyses (Fig. 1j,k). DKD tissue showed marked proximal tubule disruption with abundant injured epithelial cells. High-resolution views revealed key features: a healthy interlobular artery (with VSMCs, endothelial cells and fibroblasts; Fig. 1l), an inflamed tubulointerstitial region (with immune cells, injured epithelium and fibroblasts; Fig. 1m) and a collecting duct (CD) with principal and intercalated cells (Fig. 1n).

### Niches decode tissue architecture
The complex three-dimensional architecture of the kidney underlies its diverse functions, from glomerular filtration to medullary urine concentration. Although key structures are well characterized, we hypothesized that spatial transcriptomics could reveal additional patterns of cellular organization, which we refer to as ‘niches’ throughout this study.
Without a defined spatial scale for kidney niches, we aggregated cell–cell relationships within 20–80 µm radii to capture multiscale context and used machine learning to identify 11 recurring cellular neighbourhoods (niches) that were observed consistently across Xenium and CosMx samples (Fig. 2a,b). This unbiased analysis aligned with known kidney architecture: the glomerular niche contained glomerular podocytes, endothelial, parietal and mesangial cells and was enriched for genes linked to nephrotic syndrome and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (Fig. 2b,e–g,n). The vascular niche consisting of endothelial and VSMCs showed enrichment for muscle contraction and calcium regulation pathways (Fig. 2b,e–g,n). Tubular regions separated into PT, DCT, CNT, LOH, CD and injury-associated iPT and injured loop of Henle (iLOH) niches. The PT niche featured transport and lipid metabolism genes, whereas the iPT niche was enriched for apoptosis and STAT3 signalling (Fig. 2b,e–m). The full list of niche-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) is presented in Supplementary Tables 6–8.

To validate niche patterns, we applied the COVET framework, which models the local gene–gene covariance for each cell21. Pairwise niche distances visualized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) showed clear separation by niche labels, supporting the biological relevance of our assignments (Extended Data Fig. 3m–o). We also used NicheCompass22, a deep learning model integrating gene expression and spatial context, which identified eight niches consistent with our annotations and revealed niche-specific programmes such as APOE in PT and IL-32 in iPT (Extended Data Fig. 3p–r and Supplementary Fig. 11). Unbiased spatial mapping of cellular neighbourhoods (niches) revealed disease-associated changes in tissue organization. Niche frequencies differed markedly between healthy and disease samples (Fig. 2c) and DCT (r = 0.61) and PT (r = 0.52) correlated positively with eGFR, whereas immune (r = –0.59) and iPT (r = –0.55) niches correlated negatively with eGFR (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3a–k). These compositional shifts reflect loss of epithelial integrity and inflammation, suggesting potential utility as quantitative indicators of kidney function and disease severity.

Indeed, we found niche-specific alterations linked to extracellular matrix remodelling, adhesion, inflammation and neovascularization within the vascular niche (Fig. 2o and Supplementary Table 9). As DKD primarily affects the glomerulus, we investigated changes in cellular composition within the glomerular niche. Mesangial (P < 0.05; Fig. 2p) and immune cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 2q) were modestly increased in DKD, consistent with hallmark features of disease pathology24. Notably, these changes were correlated (r = 0.32, P < 0.05), suggesting coordinated expansion of mesangial and immune populations (Extended Data Fig. 2l). Differential correlation analysis further revealed increased mesangial–immune interactions, including COL3A1–ITGB8, CHI3L1–JUN and IGFBP5–CD24 (Extended Data Fig. 6d). The scale of our dataset enabled analysis of niche-level cell–cell interactions across disease severity. Using CellPhoneDB, we found that NRG1–ERBB3 and CXCL9–DPP4 interactions correlated positively with kidney function, whereas PLAU–PLAUR and IL-6–IL-6R signalling between glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes were enriched in severe DKD (Fig. 2r and Extended Data Fig. 6g–l), highlighting candidate pathways for future mechanistic studies.
In conclusion, spatial transcriptomics revealed kidney niches through complementary annotation- and gene expression-based approaches, recapitulating known structures and uncovering new functional domains. This niche-based framework offers a molecular lens to define disease-associated changes in composition and signalling, and potential therapeutic targets.

### The profibrotic microenvironment in DKD
Beyond identifying spatial niches, spatial transcriptomics captures how a cell’s state and gene expression vary with its immediate surroundings—the ‘microenvironment’ (Fig. 3a). Unlike multicellular niches, microenvironments reflect local context-dependent changes within a specific cell type. We focused on injured tubular (iPT and iLOH) and immune cell microenvironments to understand how local interactions contribute to disease progression.

Using neighbourhood composition, we identified five distinct injured tubular microenvironments, each defined by unique neighbouring cell types and transcriptional states (Fig. 3g–l, Extended Data Fig. 7a–e and Supplementary Tables 12–15). The proximal tubular microenvironment, enriched for PT and DCT neighbours, showed relatively healthy iPT and iTAL centre cells expressing CUBN, APOE, GPX3, EGF and COL6A1. The distal microenvironment, enriched for TAL, DTL/ATL, PC, CNT and IC neighbours, featured UMOD+/EGF+ iTAL and KRT19+/SLC39A8+ iPT cells. The periglomerular microenvironment, adjacent to glomerular cells, showed SPOCK2+/DUSP1+/IGFBP7+ iPT and CCN2+/BCAM+ iTAL cells at the centre, suggesting early stress and matrix remodelling. The perivascular microenvironment, enriched for EC_DVR and VSMC neighbours, displayed SERPINA1+ and PFKFB3+ iPT and iTAL centre cells, consistent with hypoxia-driven metabolic reprogramming. Finally, the profibrotic microenvironment, rich in immune and fibroblast neighbours, featured MMP7+/HAVCR1+/HIF1A+ iPT and MMP7+/ANXA2+/CLU+/ITGB6+ iTAL centre cells, indicating proinflammatory and profibrotic activation consistent with previous reports. Notably, ITGB6 has previously been reported as upregulated in iTAL cells25, and MMP7 has been proposed as both a biomarker of kidney disease and an indicator of renal injury26,27. Moreover, these findings are consistent with recent reports of HAVCR1+ iPT cells localizing near fibrotic regions28–30.

The profibrotic microenvironment was markedly expanded in DKD (Fig. 3d) and negatively correlated with eGFR (r = –0.6, P < 0.001; Fig. 3c). Differential expression analysis of aggregated microenvironment profiles using cell states mapped from snRNA-seq through the scANVI latent space revealed enrichment of inflammatory and fibrotic pathways—including, for example, focal adhesion or IL-7 signalling (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Tables 16–18). Spatial validation using Sirius Red staining on an adjacent section confirmed colocalization with COL1A1 deposition. Consistent with this, fibroblasts in this microenvironment showed higher COL1A1 expression than in other injured tubular microenvironments (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g).
We next analysed ligand–receptor interactions between iPT cells and fibroblasts (Supplementary Table 19) using HAVCR1 as a marker of injury. HBEGF–EGFR and TNFSF10–TNFRSF10B interactions correlated positively with HAVCR1 expression (Fig. 3p and Extended Data Fig. 8a,c), suggesting that stromal cells promote epithelial injury through EGFR and death receptor signalling31. In relation to COL1A1, a fibrosis marker, JAG2–NOTCH and PDGFβ–PDGFRβ interactions were linked to matrix production via Notch and PDGF pathways32 (Fig. 3o and Extended Data Fig. 8a,c). Bulk tubulointerstitial RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 843 kidney samples confirmed positive correlations between these pairs and HAVCR1 or COL1A1, supporting these findings (Extended Data Fig. 7n,o). Together, these results identify a disease-associated profibrotic injured tubular microenvironment that is enriched in fibroblasts and immune cells and marked by proinflammatory and profibrotic signalling.

### Spatial mapping of the immune landscape
Building on our identification of injured tubular cell states shaped by their microenvironment, we next focused on immune cells—whose functional diversity, spatial organization and transcriptional heterogeneity have historically been difficult to resolve owing to the limited spatial resolution of earlier technologies.
For this purpose, we generated a comprehensive spatially resolved human kidney immune cell atlas. Unbiased clustering identified 11 immune populations, including T cell, natural killer (NK) cell, dendritic cell, B cell, plasma, macrophage, basophil/mast and neutrophil subsets (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 3 and Extended Data Fig. 9a,b). Immune cells were increased in DKD, with plasma and B cells showing 3.1-fold and 2.4-fold higher abundance, respectively (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 9c). Spatial mapping in DKD revealed that whereas macrophages and plasma cells were broadly distributed, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and especially B cells, showed localized clustering; B cells also had the highest proportion of immune neighbours, indicating enrichment within dense immune microenvironments (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Fig. 10c).

To define the spatial organization of immune cells, we clustered them by their local cellular context (Fig. 4d). This revealed microenvironments enriched in fibroblast, injured tubular, vascular or glomerular neighbours—termed fibro, injured tubular, vascular and glomerular immune microenvironments (Fig. 4f,i–k,m,p–r). We also identified two immune-dense microenvironments: a T cell-predominant niche composed mainly of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and a B cell-predominant niche enriched in B cells with some CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4h,l,o,s).
One identified immune microenvironment contained few neighbouring immune or parenchymal cells and was termed the residential kidney immune microenvironment (Fig. 4e,g). It consisted mainly of macrophages with some CD8+ T cells. Because tissue-resident immune cells are difficult to identify spatially, previous studies proposed that immune cells in histologically normal regions are likely to represent resident populations33. Consistent with this idea, in two healthy paediatric kidneys, most immune cells localized to this compartment, which remained proportionally stable and compositionally preserved in DKD (Extended Data Fig. 9d,e and Supplementary Fig. 12a–h).
We next examined macrophages, the main cell type in the residential immune microenvironment (Extended Data Fig. 9e). In both CosMx and Xenium datasets, residential macrophages showed tissue-resident and anti-inflammatory signatures, including MRC1, CD163, ZFP36 (CosMx), MRC1, GPR34, STAB1, ALDOB and RNASE1 (Xenium). By contrast, non-residential macrophages expressed higher levels of inflammatory and antigen-presentation genes such as LYZ, S100A4, S100A6 (CosMx), ITGAX and STAT1 (Xenium) (Extended Data Fig. 10a,b).
Unbiased analysis revealed increased immune infiltration within the glomerular niche in DKD (Fig. 2q). Even healthy kidneys contained a small population of macrophages (Fig. 4t), but in DKD both macrophages and CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in number (P < 0.05). Leveraging our large cohort, we analysed ligand–receptor interactions across disease severity (Fig. 4u). The mesangial FN1–macrophage ITGA4 or ITGB7 interaction negatively correlated with eGFR, consistent with fibrotic remodelling and potential homing of macrophages34, whereas the interaction of podocyte BMP4 with BMPR2 on CD8+ T cells correlated positively with eGFR, suggesting a potential anti-inflammatory role35.
We next examined the injured tubular immune microenvironment to better understand inflammation linked to tubular injury. Even healthy kidneys contained some injured tubules (Figs. 3b and 4e) consistent with previous observations36, but in DKD this microenvironment showed increased numbers of plasma cells (P = 0.008; Fig. 4v). Ligand–receptor analysis revealed reduced ANXA1–FPR3 signalling, suggesting impaired inflammation resolution, and stronger CXCL12–CXCR4 and TNFSF13–TNFRSF13B interactions, consistent with plasma cell recruitment and survival (Fig. 4w). Bulk RNA-seq from 843 kidneys confirmed positive correlations between these interactions and the plasma cell marker XBP1 (Extended Data Fig. 10e–n).
In summary, we generated a comprehensive, spatially resolved immune cell atlas of the human kidney, integrating cell type identity, gene expression and spatial context across health and disease. This identified a stable tissue-resident immune cell organization dominated by anti-inflammatory macrophages. In DKD, we observed marked increases in B cells and plasma cells forming a highly organized B cell-predominant microenvironment that can be revealed by spatial transcriptomics.

### B cell microenvironment marks DKD subset
In DKD, B cell numbers were markedly increased and exhibited the highest degree of spatial organization among immune cell types. In a subset of patients, these B cells further assembled into a distinct, DKD-associated B cell-predominant immune microenvironment.
The B cell-predominant immune microenvironment was embedded within the profibrotic tubular niche (Figs. 3 and 5c), linking B cell accumulation to regions of injury and fibrosis. This region showed strong spatial co-expression of CXCL13–CXCR5 (Fig. 5o), a chemokine axis that organizes B and T follicular helper (TFH) cells in secondary lymphoid organs, suggesting the presence of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs). Supporting this, CD4+ T cells expressed CXCR5 and PDCD1, consistent with a TFH-like phenotype37 (Extended Data Fig. 10d), and TNFSF13B–TNFRSF13C signalling indicated active B cell survival and differentiation (Fig. 5o), implying a TLS-like niche sustaining local B cell responses in DKD.

To validate these findings at the protein level, we performed imaging mass cytometry (IMC) on adjacent tissue, confirming the presence of abundant CD20+ B cells and CD4+ T cells within this microenvironment, whereas CD8+ T cells and plasma cells were rare (Fig. 5a–n). Integration with a single-cell RNA-seq atlas38 identified naive, memory and atypical memory B cells (Fig. 5p–r), with a marked enrichment of IgD+ naive B cells (P = 2.38 × 10−11; Extended Data Fig. 12). IMC for CD27, CD11c and BCL6 supported this classification (Fig. 5s–u), and Ki-67 staining showed proliferating CD20+ B cells but not CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5m,n). Finally, Xenium detected expression of AICDA (Fig. 5g; required for class-switch recombination39) and IRF4 (Fig. 5f; essential for plasma cell differentiation40), indicating local B cell activation and differentiation into antibody-producing cells within the kidney.

To assess the effect of intrarenal B cell aggregation and immune complex formation, we derived a spatial gene signature for the B cell-predominant niche and applied it to bulk RNA-seq data from the Susztaklab Biobank to identify B cell-rich samples. IgA immunoprecipitation followed by tandem mass tag-based mass spectrometry revealed strong enrichment of immune effector proteins, including granzymes and complement components (C3, C4A and C8B), which are known contributors to tissue damage in inflamed organs41 (Fig. 5x).
We next assessed the link between B cell-predominant inflammation and kidney disease severity. Applying our spatial B cell signature to bulk RNA-seq data from 843 kidney samples in the Susztaklab Biobank revealed a strong positive correlation with interstitial fibrosis (Extended Data Fig. 11m). Using the TRIDENT cohort, we stratified patients into B+ DKD (8.4%) and B− DKD (91.6%) groups based on this signature (Supplementary Table 24). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with B+ DKD reached kidney failure end-points significantly faster over three years (Fig. 5v).

To identify biomarkers of the B cell-predominant inflammatory subset, we compared bulk RNA-seq profiles of B+ and B− DKD patients. B+ DKD resulted in higher expression of B cell markers (CD19, CD79A and PAX5), TLS-associated chemokine (CXCL13, LTA and LTB) and plasma cell (MZB1, CD38 and PRDM1) genes. We also found upregulation of BAFF/APRIL receptor genes (TNFRSF13C and TNFRSF13B) and TFH markers (CXCR5, PDCD1 and CD40LG), consistent with the spatial enrichment of these genes in the B cell-predominant microenvironment.
To identify circulating biomarkers that distinguish B+ from B− DKD, we trained an elastic-net logistic regression model on paired plasma proteomic data from TRIDENT, using SMOTE for class balance and fivefold cross-validation. The model selected 14 proteins (Fig. 5y), termed the B+ DKD protein set, which improved kidney outcome prediction beyond clinical features in Cox models (Fig. 5z, Supplementary Table 26 and Supplementary Fig. 13). External validation in 3,309 UK Biobank participants with diabetes confirmed superior performance over the kidney failure risk equation (area under the curve (AUC) = 0.70 versus 0.63; Supplementary Fig. 13a). Patients in the top quartile of the B+ DKD protein score had a substantially higher risk of progression (Supplementary Fig. 14b), suggesting potential clinical utility for identifying candidates for B cell-targeted therapy.

### Discussion
In this study, we used Xenium and CosMx to build a single-cell spatial atlas of healthy and DKD human kidneys, enabling spatial mapping of cell types and defining computationally derived, biologically validated kidney niches. In DKD, we identified disease-associated niches enriched in injured tubular and immune cells. High-resolution analysis revealed a profibrotic injured tubular microenvironment with distinct cell states and signalling networks, within which a B cell-predominant microenvironment emerged in a subset of patients. A spatial gene signature of the B cell-predominant microenvironment was associated with rapid disease progression and linked to candidate tissue and plasma biomarkers.
The complex spatial organization of the kidney demands high-resolution mapping, and here we present the most comprehensive single-cell spatial dataset of the human kidney to date42. Whereas previous studies were limited by spot-level resolution or small sample size25,28,43–47, our work integrates CosMx 1k and Xenium 5k spatial transcriptomics with single-nucleus RNA-seq, enabling cross-platform, cross-modality analysis. This integration provides a harmonized, multimodal map of human kidney cell types at unprecedented resolution.
We present a roadmap for next-generation molecular cartography using the human kidney as a model. High-resolution single-cell spatial transcriptomics, combined with complementary computational approaches, defined 11 distinct kidney niches—including both canonical and previously underappreciated compartments—consistent across platforms and samples. These niches reveal how disease reshapes tissue architecture by expanding injured and immune compartments while depleting homeostatic ones such as the DCT niche. Anchoring disease-associated signalling networks in spatial context, this framework lays the foundation for a standardized digital kidney atlas to advance cross-study comparison and precision medicine.
We introduced the concept of microenvironments—clusters of a given cell type defined by their local cellular neighbourhoods—to capture how spatial context shapes cellular states. Applying this framework to injured tubular epithelial cells revealed that tubular injury in DKD occurs within five distinct microenvironments, each with unique neighbouring cell types and gene expression profiles. The profibrotic microenvironment emerged as a key disease-associated niche, enriched in fibroblast and immune neighbours, expanded in DKD, and negatively correlated with kidney function. It contained HAVCR1+, MMP7+ injured PT and ITGB6+ LOH cells, both previously implicated in kidney disease25,36,48–51 with fibroblasts expressing high levels of COL1A1. Ligand–receptor analysis implicated epithelial–stromal crosstalk via HBEGF–EGFR, TNFSF10–TNFRSF10B, JAG2–NOTCH and PDGFβ–PDGFRβ pathways, identifying this microenvironment as a focal unit of fibrosis and disease progression.
The creation of a high-resolution spatial atlas of human kidney immune cells marks a major advance in renal immunology52. Despite recognition of immune involvement in DKD53–55, a comprehensive map of immune cell types, states and spatial organization has been lacking. Our atlas fills this gap by capturing the full spectrum of immune populations and their spatial context across healthy and diseased kidneys. Integrating spatial transcriptomics with multiplex imaging and bulk multiomics, it provides an unprecedented framework for dissecting renal inflammation at cellular resolution.
One of the most notable findings from our atlas is the marked expansion of B cells and plasma cells in DKD. Although immune dysregulation in kidney disease is well known, B cell organization had not been fully appreciated56–60. In DKD, plasma cells localized near injured tubules, whereas B cells formed a distinct TLS-like immune microenvironment60–62 embedded in the profibrotic niche, featuring Ki-67+ B cells with AICDA and IRF4 expression, strong CXCL13–CXCR5 and BAFF–BAFFR signalling and CXCR5+/PDCD1+ TFH cells. This structure, typical of autoimmune or transplant rejection settings63,64, contained predominantly memory and IgD+ naive B cells. IgA pull-down mass spectrometry from B cell-rich tissue revealed granzymes and complement proteins within immune complexes, suggesting that local antibody production contributes to inflammation and tissue injury. Notably, this B cell-predominant microenvironment defines a new therapeutic axis in DKD. Its spatial gene signature correlated with disease severity, identified a rapidly progressive DKD subtype, and yielded distinct tissue and plasma biomarkers. Although B cell-targeted therapies are established in autoimmunity and cancer, they remain unexplored in DKD. Our findings highlight the need for clinical trials testing such therapies, with biomarker-based stratification to identify patients that are most likely to benefit.
In summary, spatial single-cell transcriptomics transformed kidney anatomy into a computable disease map, revealing how DKD reshapes tissue into profibrotic microenvironments and a B cell-predominant TLS-like niche marking a progressive subset. By compressing these patterns into a tissue signature and plasma panel, we improved detection and risk stratification beyond clinical models. These results offer a blueprint for translating spatial biology into practice, defining actionable microenvironments and biomarkers to guide targeted therapy.
