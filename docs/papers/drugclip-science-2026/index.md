# Deep contrastive learning enables genome-wide virtual screening

> **Bibkey** `Jia_2026` · **Venue** Science (2026) · **Category** drug-discovery · **Relevance** high · **Access** paywall
> **Link** <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ads9530> · `status: complete`

---

## One-liner
DrugCLIP recasts virtual screening from pairwise-scoring regression into a **dense-retrieval** problem: contrastive learning embeds protein pockets and molecules into one vector space, the compound library can be **pre-encoded**, and screening needs only one pocket embedding plus cosine-similarity retrieval, taking complexity from O(M×N) to O(M+N) — up to 10 million times faster than docking per the Science abstract. It beats docking and deep-learning baselines on DUD-E and LIT-PCBA; in the wet lab it reached a 15% hit rate for NET (norepinephrine transporter) with two inhibitors' complex structures solved, and a 17.5% hit rate for TRIP12 — a target with no holo structure and no known small-molecule binders — using only AlphaFold2-predicted structures. The screening results for ~10,000 human proteins against 500 million compounds are released as the open database GenomeScreenDB.

## Problem
Of the ~20,000 protein-coding genes in the human genome, only about 10% have FDA-approved drugs or documented small-molecule binders (the preprint puts it as ~90% lacking targeted intervention), leaving the "undrugged genome" as both opportunity and problem. Screening every target experimentally is impractical, so virtual screening is the only route — but both existing routes stall. **Molecular docking** needs seconds to minutes per protein–ligand pair; the paper's example is a campaign that took two weeks on 10,000 CPU cores to screen 1 billion molecules against a single target, hopeless at genome scale. **Regression-style deep learning** suffers from three problems: affinity labels from heterogeneous experimental conditions are mutually inconsistent (merging IC50/Ki values injects substantial noise), there is a distribution shift between training sets and real screening (real libraries hold far higher proportions of inactives than curated training sets), and inference speed of million-parameter models is itself a bottleneck. The paper targets speed and robustness at once.

## Method
**Core design**: two separate encoders — one for molecules, one for pockets — trained with a contrastive loss that maximizes similarity for positive pairs (a pocket and its true binder) and minimizes it for negatives (the pocket and molecules binding other targets); at screening time vector similarity (cosine) stands in for binding probability. Two immediate benefits: labels only need the relative "binds or not" relation, sidestepping affinity-value noise; and the library can be pre-encoded offline, leaving only one pocket embedding to compute online. **Two-stage training**: (1) **pretraining** — the molecule encoder is initialized from Uni-Mol and frozen while a randomly initialized pocket encoder is aligned to it by contrastive learning (the authors call this contrastive distillation). To get enough pretraining data they introduce **ProFSA (Protein Fragment-Surrounding Alignment)**: short peptide fragments cut from protein-only structures serve as pseudo-ligands and their surroundings as pseudo-pockets, and after fragment segmentation, terminal correction, neighbor removal and pocket detection this yields **5.5 million** pseudo pocket–ligand pairs from the PDB. The pretrained pocket encoder outperforms many supervised as well as physical and knowledge-based models on downstream tasks such as pocket matching and pocket property prediction, even zero-shot. (2) **Fine-tuning** — both encoders are updated with a contrastive loss on experimentally determined protein–ligand complex structures. **Inference**: the candidate library is pre-encoded in batch by the trained molecule encoder; for a given pocket the pocket encoder produces one vector and cosine similarity retrieves the top ligands. **Adapting to AlphaFold structures**: using AF2 structures directly with Fpocket's nearest pocket drops performance sharply (DUD-E EF1% from 27.4% to 13.3%), so the authors propose **GenPack (Generation-Packing)**: detect initial pockets with Fpocket on the AF2 structure, strip side chains, run a generative model conditioned on the pocket backbone to create molecules (not necessarily synthesizable — their purpose is to localize the pocket more precisely and induce a more suitable pocket conformation), then reintroduce side chains and refine with physical force fields. **Genome-scale pipeline**: pockets for each AF2 structure are defined jointly by homology alignment (TM-align), pocket-detection software (Fpocket) and generative AI models; after retrieval, a CADD cluster-docking pipeline selects chemically diverse and physically proper molecules for each pocket.

## Data
**Pretraining**: 5.5 million pseudo pocket–ligand pairs built from the PDB with ProFSA. **Fine-tuning**: experimentally determined protein–ligand complex structures (the preprint draws these from the BioLiP2 database). **In-silico benchmarks**: DUD-E (22,886 actives across 102 targets) and LIT-PCBA (~8,000 actives plus an order-of-magnitude larger inactive set). Baselines include the physics-based docking programs Glide-SP, AutoDock Vina and Surflex, and the regression-style ML models NNScore, RFScore, Pafnucy, OnionNet, PLANET, Gnina and BigBind. **Wet-lab targets** (preprint): 5HT2AR (serotonin receptor 2A, an emerging antidepressant target; since its structure was in the training data the authors trained a customized model and excluded experimental structures at 90% homology to prevent leakage) and NET (norepinephrine transporter, an established target for depression and ADHD whose complex structures were only solved in 2024, making it a sterner generalization test). For 5HT2AR they screened 1,648,137 in-stock ChemDiv molecules. **Genome-scale screen**: all human proteins predicted by AlphaFold2 — ~10,000 proteins and over 20,000 pockets — against 500 million drug-like molecules from ZINC and Enamine REAL; the whole trillion-scale campaign finished in **24 hours on a single node with 8 A100 GPUs**, producing over 2 million potential hits with complex structures and docking scores.

## Key results
- **Speed**: the Science abstract says up to 10 million times faster than docking (the preprint says ~100,000× faster than conventional docking and ~100× faster than existing ML methods — mind the version difference when citing). Concretely, on LIT-PCBA with pre-encoding DrugCLIP finishes screening in **38 seconds**, against 3 days for Glide docking, 22 hours for Uni-Dock and 3 hours for the ML model PLANET. Its complexity is O(M+N) for M targets and N compounds, where most methods are O(M×N) — the fundamental reason genome-scale screening becomes feasible.
- **In-silico benchmarks**: on DUD-E and LIT-PCBA, measured by EF1% (enrichment factor in the top 1%, a recall metric), DrugCLIP improves substantially over every baseline (per-baseline values live in Fig. 2A/B rather than the text). It stays robust when the sequence-identity cutoff between test targets and training data is raised, and is more robust than Vina to pocket side-chain conformational error (RMSD 0–3 Å).
- **NET (the harder generalization test, preprint)**: 104 DrugCLIP-selected molecules were assayed at 10 µM by radio-ligand transport, and **16** inhibited by more than 60%; the Science abstract reports a **15%** hit rate, with 12 molecules binding better than the marketed drug bupropion. Two inhibitors' complexes with NET were solved (the preprint says by cryo-EM: 0086-0043 and Y510-9709, with IC50 values of 1.14 µM and 0.31 µM, both better than the widely used antidepressant bupropion at 1.5 µM). The chemical novelty: previous NET inhibitors typically rely on an aliphatic nitrogen forming a salt bridge with ASP75, whereas several hits here use a positively charged aromatic nitrogen — and structural analysis shows the aromatic rings do form more favourable interactions (0086-0043's isoquinoline ring makes a T-shaped π–π interaction with PHE72).
- **TRIP12 (new in the Science version)**: for a target with **neither a holo structure nor known small-molecule binders**, a **17.5%** hit rate using only AlphaFold2-predicted structures — the hardest piece of evidence in the "post-AlphaFold era" narrative. (This experiment is not in the preprint.)
- **5HT2AR (preprint)**: 78 compounds were primary-screened by calcium flux at 10 µM, 8 scoring as positive agonists (at least 10% of serotonin's activity); six had Ki < 10 µM in [³H]-ketanserin competitive binding; all six reached EC50 < 1 µM in NanoBit assays of β-arrestin2 recruitment. The highest-affinity V008-4481 has 21.0 nM affinity, 60.3 nM EC50 and 35.8% Emax, and binds the orthosteric, extended and side-extended regions with its indole ring, phenylpiperazine moiety and linear tail respectively (molecules engaging all three are rarely seen); another, V006-3328, has a branched topology uncommon among 5HT2AR binders.
- **The cost of AlphaFold structures and GenPack's repair**: replacing DUD-E's experimental structures with AF2 predictions plus Fpocket pockets drops EF1% from 27.4% to 13.3%; GenPack brings it back to 19.5% and raises the docking success rate from 19.1% to 38.7%. In other words, **AF2 structures are usable but not free, and still short of holo structures**.
- **GenomeScreenDB**: ~10,000 human proteins, over 20,000 pockets, more than 2 million high-confidence protein–ligand complex structures with scores, fully open. The preprint's coverage figure is **9,908 targets**, more than twice the 4,810 human proteins covered by ChEMBL 34 (UniProt lists 20,436 reviewed human proteins) — and not every ChEMBL target even has a high-affinity small-molecule binder; some have only peptide or antibody binders, or only vague results from low-quality assays. The paper illustrates its value with examples: JNK3 (a classical kinase target, where the identified molecules hydrogen-bond to the backbone of MET149 in the hinge region), SLC45A2 (an SLC-superfamily member with little pharmacology, where all database molecules bind near L374, a site important for protein stability), OR6A2 (an olfactory receptor expressed in macrophages that senses blood octanal and promotes atherosclerotic plaque formation, where predicted molecules fit the orthosteric pocket as potential anti-atherosclerosis inhibitors) and Sestrin-2 (a leucine sensor promoting cancer drug resistance, where predicted molecules bind the same pocket as leucine).

## Contributions
- Reformulates virtual screening as dense retrieval, which (a) takes complexity from O(M×N) to O(M+N), (b) uses contrastive learning's relative labels to sidestep affinity-value noise, and (c) incidentally eases the active/inactive distribution shift between training sets and real libraries.
- ProFSA: automatically builds 5.5 million pseudo pocket–ligand pairs from peptide fragments inside proteins and their surroundings, solving the data-volume problem for contrastive pretraining — a self-supervised construction needing no extra experimental data.
- GenPack: faced with AF2 structures lacking holo conformations, it recovers part of the screening power by an unconventional route — generate molecules to induce the pocket conformation, repack side chains, refine with force fields.
- The first screening campaign with more than 10 trillion protein–ligand scoring operations, covering nearly half the human genome, released as an open database — turning genome-scale virtual screening from an aspiration into a queryable resource.
- The wet lab validates more than "it finds binders": it finds mechanistically novel chemistry (aromatic nitrogen replacing the conventional aliphatic-nitrogen salt bridge at NET), confirmed structurally.

## Limitations
- **The pocket must be given up front.** The method's input is a pocket, not a protein; in the genome-scale pipeline pockets are assembled from TM-align homology alignment plus Fpocket plus generative models, and errors there propagate straight into retrieval, with no quantification of how pocket-definition error affects final hit rates.
- **AF2 structures carry a measurable cost**: EF1% 27.4% → 13.3%, with GenPack recovering only to 19.5%, still short of holo structures. "Covering the whole genome" and "being reliable on every target" are different claims; TRIP12's 17.5% is one success, not the expected hit rate of AF2-only screening.
- **Retrieval yields a proxy for binding probability, not affinity**: cosine similarity has no physical units and gives no binding pose. When the preprint actually picks molecules for assay it layers on docking score, drug-likeness and chemical diversity (that is how the 78 5HT2AR compounds were chosen), and the genome-scale pipeline still re-runs cluster-docking. **DrugCLIP is an extremely fast prefilter, not a replacement for docking.**
- **Training-data coverage bounds the capability**: fine-tuning relies on existing experimental complex structures, so performance on pocket types underrepresented there needs more systematic testing (the authors themselves note 5HT2AR was in the training set and had to be de-homologized against leakage); the preprint's identity-cutoff experiment addresses this but only on DUD-E.
- **The denominator of the hit rate is human-filtered**: NET's 15% (or the preprint's 16 of 104) is computed after candidates were picked from the top results by drug-likeness and similar criteria, so it measures the whole pipeline (model plus human selection), not the model's accuracy alone.
- The preprint is not peer-reviewed and differs from the final Science version in targets (5HT2AR vs TRIP12) and speedup (100,000× vs 10,000,000×); sources are flagged above, but key numbers should be cited from the published Science version.

## Relation to our direction
This is **key paper 2** for the project Sikander assigned, **"Autonomous scientific orchestration of virtual screening"** (a Hayat Lab Master's/HiWi topic: LLM agents + molecular foundation models + docking + active learning, autonomously planning, executing and improving screening campaigns from protein structures, applied to heart disease). Its place in the pipeline is clear: **the agent's fast prefilter**. **(1) Why it makes a good tool**: O(M+N) complexity and a pre-encodable library mean that after a one-off encoding cost **each new pocket query is nearly free** — exactly the property that matters most to an agent, whose mode of work is repeated probing (different pockets, different top-k, different library subsets), where a docking-style tool pays full compute for every probe. A sensible division of labour: DrugCLIP pulls tens of thousands of candidates from hundreds of millions in seconds → ATG-VS from `adaptiveflow-natbiotech-2026` docks those tens of thousands → AF3 competitive docking from `competitive-docking-npjdrugdiscov-2026` ranks the final few dozen. **(2) The decisions for an agent**: which pocket (one protein often has several Fpocket candidates — GenomeScreenDB's 20,000 pockets across 10,000 proteins shows the ratio), holo or AF2 structure (always holo when available — 27.4% vs 13.3% is the price), whether to run GenPack first on AF2 structures, where to cut top-k (a trade-off between retrieval recall and downstream docking budget, precisely the accounting an agent should do), and whether to train a target-specific model (the paper did for 5HT2AR, but that requires de-homologizing against leakage — a step an agent would easily skip). **(3) A sensible first milestone**: wrap DrugCLIP (or an equivalent retrieval prefilter) and a docking backend as two tools, and let the agent decide, under a fixed total compute budget, how many top-k candidates to pass from prefilter to docking, against a human-fixed k as control. Score it by the mean docking score of the final top-N, or enrichment of known actives (DUD-E/LIT-PCBA serve directly as ground truth, no wet lab needed). Far safer than starting fully autonomous, and the benchmarks already exist. **(4) Direct usability for the project's heart-disease focus**: GenomeScreenDB already holds precomputed screening results for ~10,000 human proteins, openly, so cardiac targets are quite likely already in it — the project can start by querying rather than screening from scratch; the paper's own OR6A2 example (macrophage octanal sensing promoting atherosclerotic plaques) is a ready cardiovascular entry point. **(5) Pitfalls to watch**: cosine similarity is **not** affinity, and using it as a reward signal for active learning is dangerous (the model learns to produce vectors that merely look like binders); the agent must keep a layer of physical checking in the loop (docking poses, PoseBusters-style plausibility, ADME). And DrugCLIP's capability is bounded by its training set's pocket coverage, yet an agent facing a new pocket cannot tell from the similarity score itself that the pocket is unfamiliar — **retrieval methods lack calibrated uncertainty**, which overlaps exactly with what the agent-trustworthiness direction cares about: an external verifier must judge whether a prediction is trustworthy, rather than letting the model's own score speak.
