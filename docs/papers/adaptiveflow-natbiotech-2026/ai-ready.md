## Abstract
Ultralarge virtual screenings (ULVSs) evaluate billions of molecules for drug discovery but face cost, flexibility and scalability limits. We introduce AdaptiveFlow, an open-source platform that makes ULVSs more accessible, scalable and efficient and supports artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) method development. AdaptiveFlow provides a screening-ready version of the Enamine REAL Space, to our knowledge the largest library of ready-to-dock, drug-like molecules, comprising 69 billion compounds, also available in SELFIES format. An 18-dimensional grid of molecular properties prioritizes promising chemical subspaces, with optional active learning, reducing computational costs by orders of magnitude. AdaptiveFlow integrates >1,500 docking protocols, including GPU-accelerated and ML-based methods, and achieves near-linear scaling on up to 5.6 million CPUs in the Amazon Web Services cloud. We identified nanomolar inhibitors of two disease-relevant targets, ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1. Co-crystal structures provided mechanistic insights into FSP1 inhibition. AdaptiveFlow enables drug discovery at unprecedented scale and supports the development of AI-driven methods.

### Motivation
A central challenge in early-stage drug discovery is identifying initial hit and lead compounds that bind specifically and potently. Experimental high-throughput screening (HTS) is constrained by high cost, long timelines and the need for scalable assays, and is dwarfed by chemical space: synthetically accessible drug-like small molecules are estimated to exceed 10^60, while typical HTS campaigns screen only hundreds of thousands of compounds. This is especially limiting for challenging targets such as protein–protein interaction interfaces or allosteric sites, where binding pockets are shallow, dynamic or poorly defined. Virtual screening has expanded to hundreds of millions to billions of molecules (ULVSs), dramatically reducing cost and time relative to HTS while also providing insight into binding mechanisms and structure–activity relationships. On-demand molecules with established synthetic routes have grown rapidly, with current estimates reaching up to 3 trillion compounds across multiple vendors. Three challenges remain: cost (prohibitive as databases grow beyond 1 trillion compounds), limited flexibility (docking algorithms rest on different theoretical foundations and need distinct input formats, and must be matched to diverse target classes), and insufficient scalability (many platforms are CPU-only while both CPU clusters and GPU architectures are needed).

### The platform
AdaptiveFlow is an open-source platform of three modules plus ready-made ligand libraries. The codebase was entirely rewritten in Python (the previous version was Bash), making it easier for others to join or extend the project.

- **AFLP** (AdaptiveFlow Ligand Preparation) prepares libraries of any size into ready-to-dock format: desalting, neutralization, stereoisomer enumeration, tautomer generation, protonation-state prediction, minimum-potential-energy 3D conformer computation, optional 3D validation, and conversion into target formats (over 100 output formats supported). Input formats: SMILES, SELFIES and amino-acid sequences. External tools: ChemAxon JChem 18.20.0 (called from AdaptiveFlow's own Java code through Nailgun, greatly reducing the number of Nailgun calls), Open Babel 2.3.2 and RDKit, with the user able to choose the tool per step and an open-source alternative available for every step (the previous version required non-open-source ChemAxon tools for some steps). It computes 28 physicochemical properties and molecular descriptors, including the quantitative estimate of drug likeness and logP. Quality checks: 3D potential energy via Open Babel automatically removes ligands with excessively high energy (an indication the compound was damaged or corrupted during preparation), plus optional PoseBusters plausibility checks on generated 3D conformers. User-adjustable timeout parameters skip ligands that take unusually long. Automatic tranche assignment: AdaptiveFlow can reorganize an input library (e.g. vendor SMILES) into a new multidimensional tranche format based on computed molecular properties — the previous version required the input to be tranche-formatted already.
- **AFVS** (AdaptiveFlow for Virtual Screening) runs large-scale docking campaigns with over 1,500 distinct docking protocols and supports small molecules, peptides, protein ligands, covalent binders and DNA/RNA targets. Output can be stored in Apache Parquet, usable by any Parquet-capable database or query system including Amazon Athena (serverless, over Amazon S3); AFVS can automatically post-process ATG prescreens using Athena to prepare the input for ATG primary screens.
- **AFU** (AdaptiveFlow Unity) combines ligand preparation and virtual screening into one streamlined, automated workflow, run as a package supporting approximately 1,500 docking protocols independently of job schedulers. Input: the molecule to be docked (SMILES, SELFIES or amino-acid sequence), the protein file, the docking site and the docking program; error checks are run on missing docking-choice-specific files, and a successful calculation returns the docking score and pose. AFU runs in standalone mode from the command line with a configuration file — useful for scientists who want dockings without computational expertise while having many protocols available — **or in API mode to interface directly with other programs and code, which can be useful for the ML communities that develop new methods for drug discovery**. AFUparr is the parallelized version, optimized for SLURM, scaling linearly with the number of molecules.

A "docking protocol" is defined as a specific combination of a sampling algorithm (pose prediction) and a scoring function. AdaptiveFlow supports approximately 40 docking programs — some performing both pose prediction and scoring, some only pose prediction, some only scoring — whose combinations give approximately 1,500 methods, accessible through a single command-line interface, so users need not individually configure and learn multiple docking programs. Many newly supported programs have special features such as protein–protein or RNA docking, explicit parameterization of solvents or metals, and covalent docking. ML-based docking integrated: EquiBind, DiffDock and TANKBind. GPU pose prediction: AutoDock-GPU, QVina2-GPU, QVina2-W-GPU, Vina-GPU; GPU-compatible deep-learning scoring: Gnina, NNScore 2.0, DeepBindRG, DeepAffinity. MD-based methods for pose selection, rescoring and refinement: MM/PBSA, MM/GBSA, and binding pose metadynamics (BPMD), with the OpenBPMD algorithm able to predict binding poses within 2 Å RMSD of the crystallographic complex for many systems. Optional PoseBusters checks on poses from Vina-based protocols verify chemical, intramolecular-ligand and intermolecular validity. QuickVina 2 accelerates AutoDock Vina with no loss of accuracy; profiling with the Intel VTune Profiler showed a flat execution profile (the most time-consuming function was under 20% of runtime), so compiler-level optimization was chosen over source tuning: rebuilt with the Intel oneAPI DPC++/C++ Compiler 2022.2.0.20220730 using automatic vectorization, interprocedural optimization and relaxed floating-point constraints, the final executable was 18% faster than default builds with no loss of accuracy (profile-guided optimization did not improve performance).

Parallelization and cloud: collections group around 1,000 ligands to reduce the overhead of scheduling individual ligands; subjobs are atomic units of computational work containing one or more collections, generated automatically to match the runtime requirements in the job configuration (approximately 30 min); subjobs are combined into work units sent to schedulers as job arrays. Batch systems supported: Slurm (which runs on Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure and AWS) and AWS Batch (with S3 object storage and CloudFormation setup; AdaptiveFlow can automatically generate the Docker container used for processing). Users provide a SLURM submission template which AdaptiveFlow populates at runtime with job arrays, partitions and memory, decoupling workflow logic from the specific cluster configuration and letting users adapt headers, modules or partition names without touching the Python codebase. Spot (preemptible) instances further reduce costs; AdaptiveFlow uses short subjobs, automatic rerunning of prematurely exited jobs, and AWS Batch "spot-capacity-aware" scheduling to select instances less likely to be interrupted. ARM CPUs are supported (AFLP fully; AFVS where docking programs release source or ARM binaries) and can be more cost-efficient than x86. Through this optimization the team achieved 5.6 million concurrent vCPUs; the previous version was only demonstrated to scale to 160,000 vCPUs. This level of scalability enables ULVSs of billions of compounds to complete within hours, versus weeks or months on traditional on-premise clusters.

### Enamine REAL Space (the library)
Virtual screening performance scales notably with library size, because larger screens tend to yield both higher potencies and improved hit rates. Until recently the largest publicly available ready-to-dock libraries — the 2018 version of the REAL Database and ZINC20 — each held approximately 1.5 billion molecules. The REAL Space enumerated release 2022q1-2 (accessed 15 November 2022) contains 31,507,987,117 enumerated molecules, becoming 69 billion after ligand preparation (for example because of stereoisomer and tautomer enumeration); following preparation with AFLP the ready-to-dock library comprises 68.7 billion commercially available on-demand small molecules. The compounds are synthetically accessible derivatives of 137,000 established building blocks, assembled using 167 internally validated one-pot synthetic protocols developed at Enamine; this combinatorial framework has a reported synthetic success rate of 82%, based on 386,000 experimental reactions conducted in 2021. In total 99% of compounds conform to Lipinski's Rule of Five. The library comprises 1,098,811,629 unique Murcko scaffolds. Over 62 billion molecules (74%) contain at least one chiral centre, contributing to "nonflat" chemical space; 23 billion compounds are halogenated, including 17.4 billion containing fluorine, well suited to 19F NMR-based binding assays that do not require isotopically labeled proteins.

Preparation (JChem Suite 18.20.0 for neutralization, stereoisomer enumeration, tautomer generation, protonation-state prediction, 3D coordinate calculation and property computation; Open Babel 2.3.2 as backup for protonation states, 3D coordinates and format conversion; the pip-installable SELFIES package from https://github.com/aspuru-guzik-group/selfies for SMILES↔SELFIES) computed 28 properties per compound. A subset of 18 key molecular properties constructs an 18-dimensional matrix: molecular weight, octanol–water partition coefficient (logP), hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor counts, rotatable bond count, topological polar surface area (TPSA), aqueous solubility (logS), aromatic ring count, molecular refractivity, formal charge, positive and negative charge counts, fraction of sp3-hybridized carbons, number of chiral centres, halogen and sulfur atom counts, and stereoisomer count (the Fig. 2 property list also names aromatic proportion, double-bond stereoisomer count and Enamine compound class). Each dimension is discretized into multiple property intervals, giving a combinatorial matrix of 24.4 billion potential tranches (matrix elements), of which REAL Space molecules occupy approximately 12 million, allowing highly granular property-based selection of compound subsets. The 18 properties were chosen to balance chemical relevance with computational efficiency, as excessive dimensionality would cause sparsity and reduce tractability. Tranche occupancy counts span several orders of magnitude, from single-molecule tranches to tranches exceeding 10 million molecules, with an average of 5,600 molecules per populated tranche; only a small fraction of theoretical grid cells is populated.

Two interactive web interfaces (built with different rendering frameworks) project the 18-dimensional matrix onto a 2D matrix whose two axes the user selects, while the ranges of all 18 dimensions can be set interactively and independently of the visualization with sliders — effectively selecting multidimensional rectangles within the 18-dimensional matrix — with the number of selected molecules updating automatically; selected subsets can be used directly for virtual screens through AFVS.

Formats: ready-to-dock PDB, PDBQT, MOL2 and SDF, compatible with most structure-based docking programs, at approximately 50 TB compressed per format, around 200 TB compressed in total (around 2 PB uncompressed), plus SMILES, SELFIES and Parquet to support cheminformatics, generative modeling and large-scale analytics. Both the full library and user-defined subsets are accessible through the AdaptiveFlow project homepage or the AWS Open Data repository, enabling direct use in the cloud without local storage. AFLP prepared the REAL Space on AWS using over 5.6 million Intel vCPUs in parallel on spot instances, with the used capacity sustained without notable preemption (under 0.1% of used CPU hours interrupted) and scaling behaviour found to be perfectly linear. Despite its breadth the 18-dimensional matrix remains sparsely populated, revealing large underexplored regions; the unoccupied tranches are presented as opportunities for prospective synthesis campaigns targeting previously undruggable regions of the proteome.

### ATG-VS (Adaptive Target-Guided Virtual Screens)
Rationale: the fraction of compounds in an ultralarge screen that will optimally engage a specific site is often very small and shares specific molecular characteristics, so to be cost and time effective it is desirable to focus the screen on the chemical space likely to contain hits for a given target. The steps are:

1. **Library decomposition.** A tranche in the current library has approximately 5,600 molecules on average; for each tranche up to ten representative ligands are selected for a pilot screen. The number of representatives per tranche (1–10) is user-selectable. With approximately 12 million populated tranches, one representative per tranche means approximately 12 million molecules in the prescreen for the entire library. If more than one representative is chosen, the additional molecules are automatically selected to be diverse from each other within the tranche.
2. **Screening representative molecules (ATG prescreen).** Instead of screening the whole library, only the selected representatives are docked. Optionally, a dynamic filtering procedure on the 18-dimensional ligand properties can be applied: users manually set criteria that molecules or tranches must satisfy, for example based on the properties of the targeted site, which can greatly reduce the number of dockings in the prescreen. After the prescreen, the subspace (tranches) with the best docking scores among representative ligands is selected for the next stage.
3. **Selection of active tranches.** AdaptiveFlow automatically selects the most promising tranches on the basis of the ATG prescreen docking scores (and ligand efficiency); the total number of ligands for the next stage is user-specified. Two selection strategies: individual tranches from the 18-dimensional matrix can be selected manually, giving fine-grained control over specific chemical subspaces; or selection can be restricted to a hyperrectangular region of the grid, obtained by identifying the top-performing intervals (bins) within each of the 18 property dimensions and computing their Cartesian product to form a structured, intersecting subset of tranches — enabling chemically diverse yet targeted selection based on multiple favorable properties. In both modes AdaptiveFlow automatically prioritizes the tranches containing the best-performing representative ligands.
4. **Active cell screening (ATG primary screen).** The tranches selected after the prescreen are fully screened (typically 1M–1B compounds). To refine this further, an ML classification model identifies the most promising molecules within each tranche: it is trained on Morgan fingerprints (size 1,024) of the prescreened compounds using the prescreen docking scores as labels, applying threshold-based classification with the 75th percentile of docking scores as the decision boundary; only molecules classified as high-confidence binders are prioritized for full docking.

An optional third-stage screen can rescore the best hits with higher accuracy, such as by including receptor flexibility, and AdaptiveFlow can carry out all three stages in a fully automated workflow. The platform also supports funnel-type strategies, standalone or combined with ATG-VS: screening a subset (or the full library where feasible) for an initial ranking, then rescreening the top candidates with increased exhaustiveness or different scoring functions, with an arbitrary number of stages. Users can also manually select library subsets by molecular properties or build smaller custom libraries with AFLP. New and/or proprietary libraries can be prepared with ATG-VS support, which requires them to be structured into a multidimensional tranche matrix with preassigned representative molecules per tranche.

**ML classifier for tranche prioritization (Methods).** An optional ML classification step in the primary screening stage prioritizes compounds most likely to be high-affinity binders, reducing the number of molecules fully docked. It is applied after the ATG prescreen to filter undocked molecules within the selected tranches. The classifier is a fully connected feedforward neural network. Compounds are represented by Morgan fingerprints of length 1,024 and radius 2, generated by RDKit 2023.03.1. The model is trained on docking scores from all representative molecules used in the prescreen, converted to binary labels by percentile thresholding: the top 25% of compounds with the most negative docking scores are positives ("high-confidence binders"), the rest negatives; the majority class is undersampled during training to mitigate class imbalance. Architecture: four fully connected layers — an input layer of 1,024 nodes, then hidden layers of 512, 256 and 128 nodes with ReLU — and an output layer of a single sigmoid-activated node giving the predicted binding probability. Hyperparameters and layer configurations were selected by limited tuning with cross-validation on the prescreen data. Training uses the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001), binary cross-entropy loss and early stopping on validation loss with a patience of five epochs; a 10% validation split, training capped at 50 epochs and a batch size of 256, though convergence typically occurs earlier. Once trained, the model is applied to all undocked compounds within the selected tranches; those with predicted binding probability greater than 0.5 are retained for full docking and the rest filtered out. This filtering step typically reduces the number of compounds subjected to docking by 30–70%, depending on the target, while maintaining or improving hit enrichment. The module is fully integrated into the AFU environment and can be activated through a configuration parameter; training and inference are implemented in PyTorch 2.0.1 with GPU acceleration.

**Closed-loop active learning.** An optional active-learning component layered on ATG-VS iteratively refines the search with data-driven feedback, dynamically selecting molecules predicted to yield the highest information gain or binding likelihood. Because each screening stage generates new labeled data, this cycle can be repeated for an arbitrary number of rounds, retraining the model on the newly docked and, where available, experimentally assayed compounds and re-selecting the next batch to screen, forming a closed-loop active-learning cycle that progressively concentrates sampling on the most promising regions of chemical space.

### Benchmarks
**Prescreen parameters.** Multiple ATG prescreens were run on an array of targets to probe the number of representatives and docking exhaustiveness (how extensively the ligand conformational space is sampled): (1) one representative molecule per tranche with exhaustiveness 1; (2) ten representatives with exhaustiveness 1; (3) one representative with exhaustiveness 10. The results — the distribution of top docking scores and the ranking of tranches within each property category — were relatively similar for all three scenarios, so ATG prescreens with exhaustiveness 1 and one representative per tranche are expected to be sufficient for many applications. In that case the prescreen docks 12 million molecules to explore a library of 69 billion compounds, more than 5,000-fold less computational effort than screening all molecules in the library. If a second-stage screen with higher accuracy is added in a multistage setup, increasing exhaustiveness may be beneficial.

**Setup.** Benchmarking covered ten diverse protein targets including kinases, phosphatases, G-protein-coupled receptors and protein–protein interaction interfaces (listed in Supplementary Table 7); Fig. 4 labels them KRAS-G12V, CB1, PTPRB, JNK3, TLR8, PIK3CG, NTR1, MED15-KIX, KEAP1 and RPTO. Two settings were used: test-based benchmarks on a 5-million-compound subset of chemical space, and large-scale production runs using the full 69-billion-compound REAL Space. For the test-based benchmarks, synthetic reactions were randomly selected from the Enamine space to create a subset totaling approximately 5 million molecules, with a different random subset for each of the ten targets to ensure unbiased evaluation across targets and screening scenarios; these benchmarks systematically assessed standard ULVS (random selection), ATG without active learning, and ATG augmented with the optional ML-based active-learning component. The full-scale production benchmarks used the entire 69-billion-compound library but without the active-learning step, to minimize computational overhead while validating ATG-VS performance at scale. All docking in these benchmarks used QuickVina 2 with default parameters unless otherwise specified; for the 100K ATG configuration, primary-screen tranches were selected with the hyperrectangle method, enabling balanced, property-informed sampling.

**(i) Comparison to standard ULVS (production-scale).** Top-50 docking scores were compared between (1) a standard ULVS of 100 million randomly selected compounds from the full Enamine REAL Space and (2) the ATG-VS method without active learning. For ATG-VS, a prescreen of 12 million compounds — ten representative ligands per tranche across the entire library — was followed by a primary screen of up to 1 million compounds; to assess the impact of screening depth, the number of compounds in the primary screen was varied systematically (10,000, 100,000 and 1 million) across the ten targets. Active learning was not applied here in order to isolate the effect of tranche prioritization alone, though on the basis of the test-based benchmarks the authors expect incorporating active learning could yield further improvements in efficiency and enrichment. ATG-VS achieved comparable or superior top docking scores to standard ULVSs while requiring dramatically fewer docking evaluations. (Extended Data Fig. 6 describes a prescreen of 11 million representative compounds followed by a focused primary screen whose size is indicated per target, with ATG consistently achieving comparable or superior docking scores using a fraction of the computational budget; Extended Data Fig. 7 compares 100 million compounds screened by standard ULVS against ATG-selected 100 million, stating the top-50 docking scores of the adaptive screens are considerably better for all target proteins.)

**(ii) Impact of ML (test-based benchmarks).** On a 5-million-compound subsample of the REAL Space, three approaches were compared: a random screen of 1 million compounds ("1M random"); ATG-VS — decomposing the library on the 18 molecular properties, running a prescreen and selecting compounds for a primary screen — with a total of 100,000 docking evaluations across both stages ("100K ATG"); and an enhanced ATG-VS in which the ML model, trained on docking scores and Morgan fingerprints from the prescreen, prioritized compounds for the primary screen ("100K ATG + AL"). In the ATG and ATG+AL cases the total number of docking evaluations including the prescreen was 100,000, versus 1 million in the random screen. The ML-augmented ATG-VS consistently improved enrichment of high-affinity virtual hits relative to standard ATG-VS, and although both ATG versions sampled only 100,000 molecules, they outperformed the random screen of 1 million.

For most target proteins, ATG primary screens (100,000 molecules) with or without active learning performed comparably to standard ULVS (1 million random compounds) — a tenfold reduction in compounds screened at similar docking performance. While ATG-based screens consistently outperformed random screening, the extent of improvement varied with the biophysical properties of the target: proteins with complex, feature-rich binding pockets, particularly those combining extensive hydrophobic surfaces with well-defined polar subpockets, derived the greatest benefit from ATG-based prioritization and ML enhancement.

**Sensitivity to the docking protocol.** To test the binning strategy against different sampling algorithms and scoring functions, the benchmarks were repeated with Smina (implementing the Vinardo scoring function) and GWOVina (incorporating flexible-side-chain docking). The physicochemical property-based binning remained effective independent of the docking protocol: ATG-VS consistently identified high-scoring compounds with speedups and enrichment factors comparable to those observed with QuickVina 2, with the average predicted binding affinity of the top 50 molecules improved relative to standard random screens while computational costs remained dramatically lower. Descriptor-based binning therefore correlates well with binding potential across docking protocols of varying sophistication.

### Experimental validation — FSP1
FSP1 is a recently characterized oxidoreductase with a central role in protecting cells from ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death triggered by membrane lipid peroxidation. FSP1 maintains pools of reduced coenzyme Q10 and vitamin K hydroquinone using NAD(P)H, operating independently of GPX4 to suppress oxidative membrane damage; inhibiting it disrupts this defense, sensitizing cells — especially redox-imbalanced cancer cells — to ferroptosis. Only a few FSP1 inhibitors have been described and structurally characterized, making it an ideal target for exploring novel inhibitor space.

**Structural work.** Ancestral sequence reconstruction — known to yield highly stable proteins while preserving functional and structural fidelity to modern homologs — produced the tetrapod ancestor of human FSP1, which shares 72.5% sequence identity and retains all active-site residues. The ancestral variant expressed efficiently in *E. coli* (~100 mg l⁻¹ culture), bound FAD, was highly thermostable (Tm = 62 °C), retained full enzymatic activity and yielded high-quality, well-diffracting crystals. High-resolution structures were determined for FSP1 bound to FAD, bound to FAD and NAD⁺, and in complex with FAD, NAD⁺ and coenzyme Q1. The overall structure is very similar to the human enzyme. NAD⁺ triggers a 2-Å shift of the flavin ring and binds in a mode fully consistent with hydride transfer for flavin reduction, the nicotinamide C4 atom sitting right in front of the isoalloxazine N5. Coenzyme Q1 binds between the aromatic side chains of Phe354 and Y290, its quinone moiety at the edge of the flavin pyrimidine ring in a canyon leading from the protein surface, with the quinone oxygen hydrogen-bonding the flavin N(3)H — a geometry enabling facile electron transfer from flavin to coenzyme Q. NAD⁺ binding is unaffected by the presence of coenzyme Q, demonstrating that coenzyme Q and NAD(P)⁺ can bind simultaneously, in agreement with the ternary-complex mechanism from the kinetics; the structure also shows ample space for oxygen to access the flavin N5 locus, explaining why FSP1 can also operate as a NAD(P)H oxidase. This experimental visualization of the NAD(P)H and quinone-binding modes provided the foundation for inhibitor discovery by in-silico screening of the substrate-binding sites.

**Screening.** FSP1 inhibitors targeting its coenzyme Q site were screened with AdaptiveFlow, using the high-resolution crystal structure of FSP1 in complex with FAD and NAD⁺ (PDB 9IFT) as the receptor. Maestro (Schrödinger) prepared the receptor by removing solvent molecules, assigning missing hydrogen bonds, correcting protonation states at pH 7.4 and assigning correct bond orders; AutoDockTools 1.5.7 converted PDB to PDBQT. The coenzyme Q site was targeted with FAD and NAD⁺ bound. In the ATG prescreen one representative compound per tranche was used (12 million dockings) and, on the basis of those results, 10 million compounds were chosen for the primary screen using ATG-VS (without the ML feature). Basic filters were applied to the top hit candidates: logP < 5, logS > −5, TPSA < 140 Å², number of stereocentres ≤ 3, and exclusion of compounds with problematic functional groups and predicted toxicity (mutagenic compounds, compounds with reproductive effects, tumorigenic compounds). In total 42 compounds from 37 different clusters with zero alerts suggested by SwissADME were ordered from Enamine, and 33 compounds were synthesized and experimentally tested.

**Results.** Of the 33 synthesized virtual hits, assessed by thermal shift and NADH depletion assays, two elicited stabilizing shifts of at least 1.5 °C in protein melting temperature and inhibited FSP1 oxidoreductase activity by at least 50% at 10 µM; both potently inhibited coenzyme Q-reduction activity, with Ki values comparable to or better than those reported for previously described FSP1 inhibitors. A cellular thermal shift assay on FSP1-expressing HEK293 cells showed that afi-FSP1-1 and afi-FSP1-2 effectively engage human FSP1 in cells, with thermal shifts of 2.8 °C and 3.1 °C respectively. afi-FSP1-1 was tested against the FSP1-expressing T cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia line Jurkat, known to undergo ferroptosis: the cells proved sensitive to the combination of afi-FSP1-1 with 0.5 µM erastin (a ferroptosis inducer), and its activity matched that of the widely used iFSP1. As a single agent afi-FSP1-1 showed no cell toxicity at concentrations up to 10 µM, and the proferroptotic effect induced by 25 µM inhibitor was completely rescued by liproxstatin-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor.

**Structure–activity relationship and mechanism.** The 3D structure of the FSP1–afi-FSP1-1 complex was used as input for an additional round of in-silico screening, which returned 20 analogs of afi-FSP1-1 that were experimentally analysed; four additional molecules (afi-FSP1-3 to afi-FSP1-6) displayed inhibitory activities, with Ki in the three-digit nanomolar range for achiral molecules and the low micromolar range for molecules tested as racemic or diastereomeric mixtures. These share an aromatic-amide-saturated-heterocycle core, as do afi-FSP1-1 and afi-FSP1-2. The crystal structure of the most potent compound (afi-FSP1-1) bound to FSP1–FAD–NAD⁺ shows the inhibitor occupying the coenzyme-Q-binding site in a mode consistent with the predicted docking pose; in its elongated conformation it contacts several aromatic side chains including Y290 and F354 (the residues that sandwich the quinone ring of coenzyme Q), with Y290 π-stacking the benzene ring of the isochroman moiety while F354 and F15 contribute hydrophobic interactions with the piperidine and π-stacking with the triazole. The central carbonyl oxygen of afi-FSP1-1 hydrogen-bonds the flavin N(3)H, as the carboxyl oxygen of coenzyme Q's ubiquinone head does, and interacts with the backbone amine (NH) of L323 through a water bridge. NAD⁺'s binding conformation is identical with the inhibitor present, in the absence of ligand and in the coenzyme Q1 complex. Crystal structures of afi-FSP1-3 and afi-FSP1-4 bound to FSP1–FAD–NAD⁺ show the same coenzyme Q site occupancy, amide carbonyl–flavin N(3)H hydrogen bond and water-mediated contact with L323; afi-FSP1-4 replaces afi-FSP1-1's dimethyltetrahydropyran/isochroman portion with a methylthiomethyltriazole yet binds very similarly through the shared phenyltriazole-piperidine moiety contacting F15 and F354, while afi-FSP1-3 adopts a less extended, U-shaped conformation lying on Y290. Binding modes were validated by mutagenesis and correlate with the measured Ki values; afi-FSP1-1 and afi-FSP1-4 have practically identical Ki while afi-FSP1-3 is twofold less potent. Cellular ADME assays indicated afi-FSP-3 is the most promising candidate for further medicinal chemistry optimization. Compounds afi-FSP1-1, -2 and -3 exhibited 10–12-fold reduced efficacy against the NADH-oxidase activity of FSP1, while afi-FSP1-4 exhibited no efficacy at all; the weaker inhibition reflects retention of NAD(P)H binding in the inhibited protein as shown by the crystal structures, indicating that reactivity with oxygen is only mildly or not affected by inhibitor binding in the coenzyme Q site. The authors posit that preferentially inhibiting the quinone-reducing activity while sparing reactivity with oxygen might be pharmacologically beneficial: inhibitors of this type may convert the enzyme from antiferroptotic (coenzyme-Q-reducing activity) to proferroptotic (uninhibited lipid-damaging ROS production). Future work is needed to explore and validate this concept in FSP1 pharmacology.

### Experimental validation — PARP1
Human PARP1 was selected as a benchmark target because of its well-characterized structure, critical role in DNA repair and relevance in cancer therapy — a mechanistically distinct, clinically validated enzyme class. Following the ATG prescreen and a primary screen of 100 million molecules, 160 promising candidates were synthesized for experimental verification. A two-concentration enzymatic HTS identified seven PARP1 inhibitors, four of which (iParp1, iParp2, iParp3 and iParp4) showed sub-250 nM IC50 values and were selected for further characterization. Structural similarity analyses confirmed that several hits shared key scaffolds with known PARP1 inhibitors while others showed different scaffold variations. iParp1 exhibited an IC50 of 8.8 nM, comparable to the FDA-approved drug olaparib — particularly notable because the hits derived from a purely virtual screen without any prior knowledge of known inhibitors or medicinal-chemistry optimization of the hit. Protein NMR confirmed direct binding to the active site of PARP1, with chemical shift perturbations supporting the predicted docking poses. X-ray crystallography revealed the 2.05-Å structure of the PARP1 catalytic domain bound to a hydrolyzed form of iParp1, validating its binding mode and key interactions. Clonogenic assays in BRCA1-deficient triple-negative breast cancer cells demonstrated selective cytotoxicity for iParp1 and iParp2, although reduced cellular activity, especially for iParp1, was attributed to poor membrane permeability because of hydrolysis.

### Discussion
The accessible chemical space is expanding at an unprecedented pace, with purchasable on-demand molecules growing from approximately 2 billion in 2020 to trillions — still a minuscule fraction of the estimated 10^60 drug-like molecules in theoretical chemical space. The authors curated the largest ready-to-dock library to date, the 69-billion-compound Enamine REAL Space, fully preprocessed in multiple formats. While ultralarge libraries increase the likelihood of potent binders and enhance true-hit rates, exhaustively screening billions of compounds imposes substantial computational cost even with today's cloud capabilities. AdaptiveFlow addresses this through ATG-VS, an efficient prescreening strategy narrowing docking to target-specific, chemically diverse subspaces enriched for high-affinity candidates: applied to the 69-billion-compound REAL Space, ATG-VS reduces screening costs up to 1,000-fold compared with exhaustive searches while preserving strong enrichment of top hits, and deep-learning-based docking plus GPU acceleration achieve a further 10–100-fold increase in throughput. Notably, whereas the authors' previous identification of nanomolar KEAP1–NRF2 inhibitors required an unbiased screen of 1.3 billion molecules, the ATG-guided screen for FSP1 achieved comparable success with just 22 million docking instances. Although the effectiveness of chemical-space focusing is target dependent, the benchmarking and experimental studies highlight ATG-VS's potential to tailor the search space to specific binding pockets, greatly enhancing screening efficiency; as chemical space continues to expand, focusing on target-specific subspaces will no longer be optional but essential. With the infrastructure in place, adding molecules to the library and directing them to their tranches is a computational process that scales linearly, and as new tranches beyond the 12 million currently populated are filled, only representatives from the newly populated tranches need to be incorporated into the prescreen set. The open-source nature of the platform enables adaptation and extension by academic and industry users. The modular architecture supports over 1,500 docking protocols encompassing scoring functions, sampling algorithms and ML-based pose predictors such as DiffDock and TANKBind; the platform natively supports SELFIES representations, physicochemical property calculations and active-learning-driven workflows, making it fully compatible with generative models and reinforcement-learning pipelines, and positioning it to rapidly evaluate designed molecules against biologically relevant structural targets.
