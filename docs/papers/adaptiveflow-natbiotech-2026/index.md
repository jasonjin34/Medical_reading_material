# AI-enhanced adaptive virtual screening of large libraries for ligand discovery

> **Bibkey** `Cecchini_2026` · **Venue** Nature Biotechnology (2026) · **Category** drug-discovery · **Relevance** high · **Access** open
> **Link** <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41587-026-03217-x> · `status: complete`

---

## One-liner
AdaptiveFlow is an open-source (GNU GPL-2.0) ultralarge virtual screening (ULVS) platform: it prepares Enamine REAL Space 2022q1-2 into 69 billion ready-to-dock molecules partitioned by 18 molecular properties into ~12 million tranches, then runs Adaptive Target-Guided Virtual Screens (ATG-VS) — a 12-million-docking prescreen over 1–10 representative molecules per tranche, followed by a primary screen of only the best tranches (optionally with an active-learning ML classifier) — cutting exhaustive docking cost by up to 1,000-fold; it integrates ~1,500 docking protocols, scales near-linearly to 5.6 million vCPUs on AWS, and prospectively produced nanomolar inhibitors of FSP1 and PARP1 (afi-FSP1-1 Ki 0.283 µM, iParp1 IC50 8.8 nM) together with co-crystal structures.

## Problem
Synthetically accessible drug-like space is estimated at >10^60 molecules while a typical experimental HTS screens only hundreds of thousands; purchasable on-demand molecules grew from ~2 billion in 2020 to trillions (the paper cites estimates reaching 3 trillion across vendors). ULVS is the alternative, but faces three hard limits: **cost** (exhaustive docking of billions to tens of billions is prohibitive even in the cloud), **flexibility** (different target classes need docking algorithms built on different theoretical foundations with distinct input formats, while platforms are locked to a few programs), and **scalability** (most existing platforms are CPU-only and cannot exploit GPUs and emerging architectures). The bottleneck is sharpest for shallow, dynamic, poorly defined pockets such as protein–protein interaction interfaces and allosteric sites. The paper asks how to **concentrate the docking budget, without exhaustive enumeration, on the small chemical subspace most likely to contain hits for a given binding site**, and how to package that as reusable open infrastructure that also serves AI/ML method development.

## Method
**Three modules**: AFLP (ligand preparation), AFVS (the screening engine) and AFU (Unity, merging both into one automated pipeline, runnable standalone from the command line or in **API mode so other programs/ML code can call it directly**; AFUparr is the SLURM-parallel version). The codebase was rewritten from Bash (previous generation) to Python.

**AFLP**: desalting, neutralization, stereoisomer enumeration, tautomer generation, protonation-state prediction, minimum-potential-energy 3D conformer generation and target-format conversion (>100 output formats); inputs are SMILES, SELFIES or amino-acid sequences; external tools are swappable step by step (ChemAxon JChem 18.20.0 / Open Babel 2.3.2 / RDKit, with an open-source option for every step — the previous version required non-open-source ChemAxon for some); quality control computes 3D potential energy with Open Babel to drop molecules whose energy is excessive (a sign of corruption during preparation) and optionally runs **PoseBusters** conformer plausibility checks; it computes **28** physicochemical properties/descriptors per molecule (including QED and logP) and **automatically** reorganizes a library into the multidimensional tranche matrix (the previous version required tranche-formatted input). For speed it calls JChem through Nailgun from its own Java code (fewer Nailgun calls) and adds user-tunable timeouts to skip unusually slow ligands.

**The 18-dimensional tranche matrix**: 18 of the 28 properties become axes (molecular weight, logP, hydrogen-bond acceptor/donor counts, rotatable bond count, TPSA, molecular refractivity, logS, aromatic ring count / aromatic proportion, formal charge, positive/negative charge counts, fraction of sp3 carbons, chiral-centre count, (double-bond) stereoisomer count, halogen- and sulfur-atom counts, Enamine compound class), each discretized into intervals, giving **24.4 billion** theoretical tranches of which REAL Space occupies **~12 million**; a populated tranche holds **5,600** molecules on average, but occupancy spans orders of magnitude (from single-molecule tranches to tranches of **>10 million**). Two interactive web interfaces (different rendering frameworks) project the 18 dimensions onto 2D while sliders set the range of all 18 independently — effectively selecting a hyperrectangle in 18-D, with the selected molecule count updating live and the subset usable directly in AFVS. The choice of 18 balances chemical relevance against tractability: more dimensions would make the matrix too sparse.

**ATG-VS in four steps**: (1) *library decomposition* — pick 1–10 representatives per tranche (user-set; with >1, representatives are automatically chosen to be diverse from each other); (2) *ATG prescreen* — dock only those representatives (1 per tranche ⇒ 12 million dockings for the whole library, **>5,000-fold** less than exhaustively docking 69 billion), optionally narrowed further by dynamic filters on the 18 properties (e.g. criteria set by hand from the properties of the targeted site); (3) *selection of active tranches* — pick the best tranches automatically from representative docking scores and ligand efficiency, either by selecting individual tranches manually (fine-grained control over specific chemical subspaces) or by taking the top-performing bin in each of the 18 dimensions and forming their **Cartesian product as a hyperrectangle** (a chemically diverse yet targeted subset); the user sets the total molecule count for the primary screen and the platform prioritizes the tranches with the best-performing representatives; (4) *ATG primary screen* — fully screen the selected tranches (typically 1M–1B compounds), optionally pre-filtering within each tranche with the ML classifier. An optional third stage rescores top hits at higher accuracy (e.g. with receptor flexibility), and all three stages can run fully automatically. Classical funnel-type multistage screening is supported too (an initial ranking, then rescreening the top with higher exhaustiveness or different scoring functions), with arbitrarily many stages; new or proprietary libraries can also be prepared by AFVS into a multidimensional tranche matrix with preassigned representatives so that ATG-VS applies.

**The optional ML / active-learning layer**: between prescreen and primary screen, a fully connected feedforward network filters the not-yet-docked molecules inside the selected tranches. Inputs are Morgan fingerprints (length 1,024, radius 2) from RDKit 2023.03.1; labels come from prescreen docking scores — the top 25% most negative (a 75th-percentile decision boundary) are positives ("high-confidence binders"), the rest negatives, with the majority class undersampled during training; the architecture is 1,024 → 512 → 256 → 128 (ReLU) → one sigmoid output; Adam (lr 0.001), binary cross-entropy, a 10% validation split, early stopping with patience 5, up to 50 epochs, batch size 256 (convergence is usually earlier); hyperparameters came from limited tuning by cross-validation on the prescreen data; it is implemented in PyTorch 2.0.1 with GPU support and integrated into AFU, **activated by a single configuration parameter**. Compounds with predicted binding probability >0.5 go to full docking, which **typically cuts the number docked by a further 30–70%** depending on the target while maintaining or improving hit enrichment. Because every screening stage produces new labeled data, the cycle can be repeated for **an arbitrary number of rounds**, retraining on newly docked (and where available experimentally assayed) compounds and re-selecting the next batch — a closed loop that progressively concentrates sampling on the most promising regions.

**Docking-protocol combinatorics**: a "docking protocol" is defined as one specific combination of *sampling algorithm (pose prediction) × scoring function*; the platform supports ~40 docking programs (three kinds: pose prediction plus scoring, pose prediction only, scoring only) freely combined into **~1,500 protocols** reachable from a single command-line interface — users need not configure and learn multiple docking programs individually. Supported ligand/target types include small molecules, peptides, proteins (protein–protein docking), DNA/RNA and covalent binders, with "special features" such as explicit solvent/metal parameterization in some programs; ML docking includes **DiffDock, TANKBind, EquiBind**; GPU scoring includes Gnina, NNScore 2.0, DeepBindRG, DeepAffinity; GPU pose prediction includes AutoDock-GPU, QVina2-GPU, QVina2-W-GPU and Vina-GPU; MD-based rescoring/refinement includes **MM/PBSA, MM/GBSA and binding pose metadynamics (OpenBPMD)**; Vina-family poses can be checked with PoseBusters. Profiling QuickVina 2 (Intel VTune) showed a flat execution profile (the costliest function was <20% of runtime), so the route taken was compiler-level: rebuilding with the Intel oneAPI DPC++/C++ Compiler 2022.2.0.20220730 using automatic vectorization, interprocedural optimization and relaxed floating-point constraints made the executable **18% faster** than default builds with no accuracy loss (profile-guided optimization did not help).

**Engineering and cloud**: parallelism has three levels — collections (~1,000 ligands, reducing per-ligand scheduling overhead) → subjobs (atomic units of work, generated automatically from the job configuration, ~30 min) → work units (submitted to the scheduler as job arrays); batch systems are **Slurm** (Google Cloud / Azure / AWS; the user supplies a submission template that AdaptiveFlow fills at runtime with job arrays, partitions and memory, decoupling workflow logic from cluster configuration) and **AWS Batch** (plus S3 object storage, CloudFormation deployment and automatic Docker-container generation); spot (preemptible) instances cut cost, with AWS Batch's "spot-capacity-aware" scheduling plus short subjobs and automatic re-running absorbing preemption; x86 and **ARM** CPUs (ARM is often cheaper) and NVIDIA/AMD GPUs are supported. AFVS output can be written as **Apache Parquet**, readable by any Parquet-capable query system (e.g. serverless **Amazon Athena** over S3), and ATG prescreens can be post-processed automatically through Athena to prepare the primary-screen input.

**The FSP1 wet-lab chain**: ancestral sequence reconstruction first gave the tetrapod ancestor of human FSP1 (72.5% sequence identity, all active-site residues retained; ~100 mg l⁻¹ in *E. coli*, Tm 62 °C, full enzymatic activity, well-diffracting), from which three high-resolution structures were solved (FSP1–FAD, FSP1–FAD–NAD⁺, FSP1–FAD–NAD⁺–coenzyme Q1); **PDB 9IFT** (FSP1 + FAD + NAD⁺) was then the receptor for screening the coenzyme Q site, prepared in Maestro (Schrödinger) by removing solvent, adding missing hydrogens, correcting protonation at pH 7.4 and assigning bond orders, and converted to PDBQT with AutoDockTools 1.5.7.

## Data
**Chemical library**: Enamine REAL Space, enumerated release **2022q1-2** (accessed 15 Nov 2022), **31,507,987,117** original unprocessed SMILES, expanded by AFLP preparation (mainly stereoisomer and tautomer enumeration) to **68.7 billion** ready-to-dock molecules (69 billion in the abstract). Composition: **137,000** established building blocks × **167** internally validated Enamine one-pot synthetic protocols; a reported **82%** synthetic success rate based on **386,000** experimental reactions in 2021; **99%** comply with Lipinski's Rule of Five; **1,098,811,629** unique Murcko scaffolds; **>62 billion (74%)** contain at least one chiral centre ("nonflat" space); **23 billion** are halogenated, of which **17.4 billion** contain fluorine (suited to ¹⁹F NMR binding assays that need no isotopically labeled protein). For scale, the largest previously public ready-to-dock libraries (the 2018 REAL Database, ZINC20) held ~1.5 billion each. Formats: ready-to-dock PDB, PDBQT, MOL2 and SDF (~**50 TB** compressed per format, ~**200 TB** for all four; ~**2 PB** uncompressed) plus SMILES, **SELFIES** and Parquet. The 18-D matrix remains sparsely populated, and the authors read the empty tranches as chemical space worth populating by prospective synthesis.

**Benchmark targets**: ten structurally diverse proteins spanning kinases, phosphatases, GPCRs and protein–protein interaction interfaces, labeled in Fig. 4 as **KRAS-G12V, CB1, PTPRB, JNK3, TLR8, PIK3CG, NTR1, MED15-KIX, KEAP1, RPTO** (exact identities and structural provenance are in Supplementary Table 7, unread). Two settings: (a) *test-based benchmarks* — a ~**5 million**-compound subset built from randomly selected Enamine synthetic reactions, with a **different** random subset per target to keep the evaluation unbiased; (b) *production-scale benchmarks* — the full 69-billion REAL Space. All benchmark docking used **QuickVina 2** with default parameters; **Smina (Vinardo scoring)** and **GWOVina (flexible side chains)** were used to re-run the benchmarks and test how the binning strategy depends on sampling algorithm and scoring function.

**Experimental targets, structures and assays**: FSP1 (coenzyme Q site, receptor PDB 9IFT) and human PARP1. Deposited structures: FSP1 **PDB 9IFZ, 9IFT, 9IFY, 9IFU, 9IFW, 9IG9, 9QRT**; the PARP1–inhibitor complex **PDB 8VYH** (catalytic domain with a hydrolyzed iParp1, 2.05 Å); the FSP1 ancestral sequence **GenBank PQ619396**. Cellular/biochemical systems: FSP1-expressing HEK293 (cellular thermal shift assay), the T cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia line **Jurkat** (with the ferroptosis inducer erastin at 0.5 µM, rescued by the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1), and **BRCA1-deficient triple-negative breast cancer** cells (PARP1 clonogenic assays). Readouts: NADH-oxidation kinetics (340 nm, pH 7.2; NADH 5–200 µM, coenzyme Q1 100 µM, FSP1 0.1–0.2 µM), thermal shift, surface plasmon resonance (Kd), an indirect resazurin assay (IC50), protein NMR, X-ray crystallography, mutagenesis and cellular ADME.

## Key results
- **Compute saved by ATG-VS**: a prescreen with one representative per tranche needs only **12 million** dockings to probe the 69-billion library, **>5,000-fold** less than exhaustive docking; the Discussion's bottom line is that ATG-VS cuts cost by **up to 1,000-fold** versus exhaustive search, with deep-learning docking plus GPU acceleration adding another **10–100-fold** in throughput.
- **The prescreen's hyperparameters have already saturated**: comparing (1) one representative at exhaustiveness 1, (2) ten representatives at exhaustiveness 1 and (3) one representative at exhaustiveness 10 gave **similar** top-score distributions and tranche rankings within each property dimension, so the authors consider the cheapest setting (one representative, exhaustiveness 1) sufficient for many applications; raising exhaustiveness pays off only when a higher-accuracy second stage is added.
- **Test-based benchmarks (5M subset, 10 targets)**: three arms — 1M random, 100K ATG, 100K ATG+AL (the latter two's 100,000 dockings **include the prescreen**, i.e. a tenth of the random screen's budget). Mean top-50 docking scores (kcal mol⁻¹): KRAS-G12V −11.83 / −11.95 / **−12.11** (brute-force best on that 5M subset: −13.11), CB1 −13.93 / −14.14 / **−14.25** (−15.20), PTPRB −9.37 / −9.60 / **−9.90** (−10.41), JNK3 −12.08 / −12.37 / **−12.54** (−13.64), TLR8 −10.38 / −10.55 / **−10.81** (−11.70), PIK3CG −11.90 / −11.99 / **−12.25** (−13.82), NTR1 −13.10 / −13.26 / **−13.58** (−14.69), MED15-KIX −9.51 / −9.77 / **−9.96** (−10.48), KEAP1 −11.30 / −11.49 / **−11.66** (−12.60), RPTO −10.92 / −11.00 / **−11.19** (−12.11). So **sampling a tenth of the molecules matches or beats a 1M random screen, and the ML step helps consistently across all ten targets**.
- **Production-scale benchmarks (full 69B library, active learning off to limit overhead)**: against a standard ULVS of 100 million random compounds, ATG-VS (prescreen of ten representatives per tranche ⇒ 12 million compounds, with primary-screen size varied over 10,000 / 100,000 / 1 million; the Extended Data Fig. 6 caption states an 11-million-representative prescreen, and Fig. 7 additionally compares 100 million versus 100 million) reached **comparable or superior** top-50 docking scores with dramatically fewer evaluations; the Fig. 7 caption goes further, stating ATG's top-50 scores are considerably better than standard ULVS for **all** target proteins tested.
- **The benefit is target dependent but the binning is protocol-insensitive**: proteins with complex, feature-rich pockets — especially extensive hydrophobic surfaces combined with well-defined polar subpockets — gained most from ATG prioritization and ML enhancement. Re-running with Smina (Vinardo) and GWOVina (flexible side chains) gave speedups and enrichment factors comparable to QuickVina 2, with top-50 mean predicted affinity again better than random screens, showing that **descriptor-based binning correlates with binding potential across docking protocols of varying sophistication**.
- **Scaling**: AFLP prepared REAL Space on AWS using **>5.6 million Intel vCPUs** in parallel with "perfectly linear" scaling, and with spot instances **<0.1%** of CPU hours were lost to preemption; the previous-generation platform was only demonstrated to **160,000** vCPUs. AFVS shares AFLP's parallelization, so its scaling is **expected** to be the same. At this scale a billion-compound ULVS finishes in **hours** rather than the weeks-to-months of traditional on-premise clusters.
- **FSP1 screen and hits**: an ATG prescreen (one representative per tranche ⇒ 12 million dockings) plus a **10 million**-compound primary screen (**no ML**); filters on the top candidates (logP < 5, logS > −5, TPSA < 140 Å², ≤3 stereocentres, excluding problematic functional groups and predicted toxicity — mutagenic, reproductive, tumorigenic); **42** compounds with zero SwissADME alerts from **37** clusters were ordered from Enamine and **33** were synthesized and tested. Thermal shift plus NADH depletion gave **2** hits (melting-temperature shift ≥1.5 °C and ≥50% inhibition of FSP1 oxidoreductase activity at 10 µM): **afi-FSP1-1, Ki 0.283 ± 0.05 µM (Kd 0.098 µM)** and **afi-FSP1-2, Ki 0.777 ± 0.22 µM (Kd 0.266 µM)**, comparable to or better than previously reported FSP1 inhibitors. CETSA in FSP1-expressing HEK293 showed **2.8 °C / 3.1 °C** shifts, i.e. engagement of human FSP1 in cells. Jurkat cells were sensitive to afi-FSP1-1 with 0.5 µM erastin, matching the widely used iFSP1; as a single agent it showed no toxicity up to 10 µM, and the proferroptotic effect at 25 µM was fully rescued by liproxstatin-1. Feeding the 3D structure of the FSP1–afi-FSP1-1 complex into **another in-silico round** returned **20** afi-FSP1-1 analogs for experimental testing, **4** of them active: afi-FSP1-3 Ki 0.520 ± 0.11, afi-FSP1-4 0.284 ± 0.07, afi-FSP1-5 1.000 ± 0.32 and afi-FSP1-6 3.940 ± 0.71 µM (the last two measured as a racemic and a diastereomeric mixture); all share an aromatic-amide/saturated-heterocycle core. Cellular ADME pointed to **afi-FSP-3** as the most promising candidate for further medicinal-chemistry optimization.
- **FSP1 mechanistic insight**: the co-crystals show every inhibitor occupying the coenzyme Q site and displacing coenzyme Q1, its (amide) carbonyl oxygen hydrogen-bonding to the flavin N(3)H exactly as the ubiquinone head does, plus a water-bridged contact to the L323 backbone NH; Y290 and F354 (the two aromatic side chains that sandwich coenzyme Q's quinone ring) sandwich the ligand and F15 contributes hydrophobic and π-stacking contacts; the binding modes were validated by mutagenesis and correlate with the measured Ki. NAD⁺'s binding conformation is identical with or without ligand and with coenzyme Q1, so afi-FSP1-1/-2/-3 are **10–12-fold** weaker against FSP1's NADH-oxidase activity and afi-FSP1-4 has no effect; the authors therefore propose, as a concept still to be validated, that **selectively inhibiting quinone reduction while sparing oxygen reactivity could convert FSP1 from antiferroptotic to proferroptotic**. The structures also show directly that NAD(P)H and coenzyme Q can bind simultaneously in catalytically competent orientations (consistent with the ternary-complex kinetics) and that oxygen has ample access to the flavin N5 — explaining FSP1's NAD(P)H-oxidase activity.
- **PARP1**: an ATG prescreen followed by a **100 million**-molecule primary screen; **160** candidates were synthesized; a two-concentration enzymatic HTS found **7** inhibitors, **4** of them (iParp1–iParp4) with IC50 < 250 nM; **iParp1 reached IC50 8.8 nM**, comparable to the FDA-approved olaparib — from a purely virtual screen **with no prior knowledge of known inhibitors and no medicinal-chemistry optimization**. Structural-similarity analysis showed some hits sharing key scaffolds with known PARP1 inhibitors and others being different scaffold variations. Protein NMR chemical-shift perturbations confirmed direct active-site binding and supported the predicted poses; X-ray gave the 2.05-Å structure of the PARP1 catalytic domain with a hydrolyzed iParp1. Clonogenic assays in BRCA1-deficient triple-negative breast cancer cells showed selective cytotoxicity for iParp1 and iParp2, but reduced cellular activity (especially for iParp1) attributed to **poor membrane permeability caused by hydrolysis**.
- **Comparison with the authors' own earlier work**: their previous nanomolar KEAP1–NRF2 inhibitors required an unbiased screen of **1.3 billion** molecules; here the ATG-guided FSP1 screen achieved comparable success with just **22 million** docking instances.

## Contributions
- **It turns "which molecules to screen" into an explicit, low-dimensional decision problem**: 18 physicochemical properties discretize the 69-billion library into ~12 million tranches, and representative docking scores score each tranche — effectively a per-target activity heat map of chemical space. Decisions move from the molecule to the tranche level, shrinking the action space from 10^10 to 10^7 while keeping it interpretable.
- **It publishes the largest ready-to-dock library as a public asset**: 69 billion molecules in four 3D docking formats plus SMILES/SELFIES/Parquet, reachable directly in the cloud through an AWS Open Data S3 bucket without local storage (raw Enamine SMILES under CC-BY); the SELFIES release serves generative models directly and the Parquet release large-scale analytics.
- **It decouples sampling algorithm × scoring function into a composable protocol space**: ~40 programs combine into ~1,500 protocols switchable from a single CLI/config file, covering protein/RNA/DNA/peptide/covalent targets, ML docking (DiffDock, TANKBind, EquiBind) and MD rescoring (MM/PBSA, MM/GBSA, BPMD), sharply lowering the barrier for non-specialists and the ML community.
- **It treats ML as a first-class citizen, not an add-on**: AFU's API mode lets docking scores serve directly as objective functions for generative/optimization models; the prescreen automatically produces labeled data (Parquet + Athena queries) and the active-learning layer can repeat arbitrarily, making screening a data generator — the authors explicitly position the platform not only for *applying* ML but for *developing* ML architectures and pipelines for structure-based drug discovery.
- **It quantifies cloud-native engineering as part of the method**: three-level parallelism (collection/subjob/work unit), ~30-minute subjobs with automatic re-running and spot-capacity-aware scheduling kept preemption losses below 0.1% across 5.6 million spot vCPUs; a Slurm submission template decouples workflow logic from cluster configuration; ARM support and compiler-level optimization (QuickVina 2 18% faster) come with numbers too.
- **End-to-end prospective validation that also yields mechanism**: two mechanistically distinct targets (the oxidoreductase FSP1 and the DNA-repair enzyme PARP1) went from a purely virtual screen to nanomolar inhibitors plus co-crystal structures plus cellular target engagement, and produced a structural explanation of FSP1 catalysis (simultaneous NAD(P)H and coenzyme Q binding, dual oxidase activity) plus the new pharmacological hypothesis that selectively inhibiting quinone reduction could invert FSP1's phenotype.

## Limitations
- **The effectiveness of chemical-space focusing is target dependent**, as the authors state; the gain is largest for complex, feature-rich pockets, and no failure analysis is given for shallow, featureless sites — how much is saved there, or whether the approach breaks down, is unknown.
- **The benchmark metric is docking score, not measured affinity**. Differences in mean top-50 docking score between arms are mostly 0.1–0.4 kcal mol⁻¹, well inside the error of the scoring functions themselves; and the "brute-force best" reference exists only on the 5-million subset — there is (and can be) no exhaustive baseline for the full 69-billion library, so "up to 1,000-fold cheaper" is quoted against a baseline **nobody actually runs**. How much it saves against a well-tuned classical funnel screen is not addressed head-on.
- **The active-learning layer was not used in the real production runs**: the production-scale benchmarks explicitly omit active learning "to minimize computational overhead", and the FSP1 primary screen is labeled "without ML feature". All evidence for the ML enhancement therefore comes from the 5-million subset, and its benefit at 69-billion scale is an **expectation** ("we expect that incorporating active learning could yield further improvements"), not a measurement; there is no causal chain between the abstract's "optional active learning" and the two wet-lab successes.
- **The classifier learns the scoring function, not activity**: labels are simply the most negative 25% of prescreen docking scores, so the layer learns to reproduce QuickVina 2's preferences and inherits that function's systematic error; both the 75th-percentile label boundary and the 0.5 probability threshold are fixed heuristics with no sensitivity analysis.
- **The granularity assumptions behind tranche decisions are untested**: the 18-D binning scheme, the "best bin per dimension × Cartesian product" hyperrectangle heuristic and the use of ligand efficiency are never compared with alternative subspace-selection methods (scaffold-based, similarity retrieval, or learned surrogates); the prescreen uses only 1–10 representatives for tranches that average 5,600 molecules and whose occupancy spans orders of magnitude (some exceed 10 million), and how much variance one representative captures is not quantified.
- **The denominator of the hit rate has been human-filtered**: FSP1 went 10 million docked → 42 ordered (37 clusters, zero SwissADME alerts plus several property and toxicity filters) → 33 synthesized → 2 primary hits; PARP1 went 160 synthesized → 7 inhibitors → 4 below 250 nM. These ratios include expert filtering and selection, so they are not a virtual-screening hit rate, and AdaptiveFlow's contribution cannot be separated from the fact that larger libraries yield better molecules anyway.
- **There is still a clear gap from hit to usable compound**: iParp1's enzymatic IC50 of 8.8 nM matches olaparib but its cellular activity is weak, attributed to poor permeability from hydrolysis; the FSP1 structural work used an ancestral protein of 72.5% sequence identity rather than the human enzyme (cellular engagement shown separately by CETSA); and the cellular ADME verdict is only that afi-FSP-3 is the best starting point for further optimization — no drug-like endpoint yet. REAL Space is also an on-demand library whose 82% synthetic success rate means some ordered candidates never materialize (here: 42 ordered → 33 synthesized).
- **Barriers and interests around the asset itself**: the ready-to-dock library is ~200 TB compressed (~2 PB uncompressed), so practical use essentially requires AWS S3; it is a single vendor's (Enamine) 2022 release while the text itself notes on-demand space is now at trillion scale; reproducing 5.6-million-vCPU runs needs cloud credits (the authors acknowledge AWS research credits and a free ChemAxon academic licence); and several authors work for or founded Enamine, Onepot AI, AWS, Google, NVIDIA, Intel and computational drug-discovery service companies (Virtual Discovery, Phind Beteiligungs UG). The direct evidence for near-linear scaling is also AFLP's; AFVS is only "expected to be the same".

## Relation to our direction
This is **key paper 1** for the Sikander Hayat lab (Uniklinik Aachen) project **"Autonomous scientific orchestration of virtual screening"** (a thesis/HiWi topic: LLM agents + molecular foundation models + docking + active learning that, given protein structures, autonomously plan, execute and improve screening campaigns and find suitable compounds; application area heart disease). Among the four readings its role is **the machine the agent has to drive**: the paper has already decomposed a ULVS campaign into parameterized, configurable, scriptable stages, and the authors explicitly designed AFU's API mode for "ML communities that develop new methods for drug discovery" — nearly every handle an agent needs already exists, so there is no need to build a screening engine.

**Parts that wrap directly into agent tools (each an interface the paper actually exposes)**: (1) `prepare` = AFLP — receptor/ligand preparation plus the 28 property calculations, accepting SMILES, SELFIES or amino-acid sequences, with a choice of tool per step; (2) `tranche_select` — manual tranche picking or the hyperrectangle method on the 18-D matrix (the semantics of the web interface's 18 sliders are exactly the constraint set an agent would emit); (3) `prescreen` = the ATG prescreen — representatives per tranche (1–10) × exhaustiveness, emitting Parquet; (4) `query` — SQL over the prescreen results with Amazon Athena (a natural agent-tool shape and the evidence source for tranche decisions); (5) `primary_screen` — full docking under a user-specified molecule budget; (6) `ml_filter` — the Morgan-fingerprint classifier toggled by one configuration parameter (the ready-made substrate for the project's "active learning", and the slot where a DrugCLIP-style contrastive scorer drops in); (7) `rescore` — the high-accuracy third stage (receptor flexibility, MM/PBSA, MM/GBSA, BPMD); (8) `pose_check` — PoseBusters physical-validity checks as an automatic sanity check; (9) `dock(protocol=…)` — one interface over ~1,500 protocols; (10) `submit` — filling the Slurm / AWS Batch submission template (job arrays, partitions, memory) and setting subjob length and spot strategy, i.e. the agent's compute-and-budget action.

**Decisions an agent could actually make (each with a quantitative basis in the paper)**: **which docking protocol** — ~1,500 combinations are a real discrete action space, and the paper shows the binning is protocol-insensitive (comparable speedups and enrichment for QuickVina 2 / Smina-Vinardo / GWOVina), so an agent can safely use a cheap protocol for the prescreen and an expensive one for rescoring, and pick programs by target class (kinase / GPCR / PPI interface / RNA / covalent); the single-calculation timings in Extended Data Fig. 9 are its cost-estimation lookup table. **Which chemical subspace** — constraints over the 18 properties are the most natural agent output, and the paper itself demonstrates the full translation from target and downstream needs into them (FSP1 used logP < 5, logS > −5, TPSA < 140 Å², ≤3 stereocentres, plus zero SwissADME alerts and diverse sampling across 37 clusters); heart-disease constraints hang off the same dimensions. **How much compute to spend** — prescreen size (12 million whole-library vs dynamically filtered), representatives per tranche (1 vs 10), exhaustiveness (1 vs 10) and primary-screen budget (the paper actually used 10,000 / 100,000 / 1 million / 10 million / 100 million) are all explicit knobs. **When to stop** — the paper supplies four nascent stopping signals: the prescreen hyperparameters have saturated (three settings give similar results ⇒ don't spend there), the residual gap in mean top-50 docking score to the brute-force reference (on the 5M subset 100K ATG+AL is still ~0.5–1.6 kcal mol⁻¹ away), the fraction removed by the ML filter (30–70%), and whether another round still improves things — the FSP1 second round (re-screening from the FSP1–afi-FSP1-1 co-crystal, 20 analogs → 4 new inhibitors) is exactly the SAR iteration an agent should learn to trigger itself. **Which molecules to order or synthesize** — cluster diversity plus property filters plus toxicity/ADME alert filters is fully automatable yet still expert-driven here, and the easiest place for an agent to add measurable value.

**A sensible first thesis milestone**: reproduce and wrap the paper's three-arm experiment in the 5-million-compound test-based benchmark setting (the recipe is complete: build the subset from randomly selected Enamine synthetic reactions, QuickVina 2 with default parameters, the arms 1M random / 100K ATG / 100K ATG+AL, mean top-50 docking score as the metric, the brute-force value as a reference upper bound, ten targets across classes) as a harness an agent can call; then have an LLM agent — **given only the receptor structure and a one-sentence natural-language goal** — emit the AFU/AFVS configuration file itself (protocol choice, the 18-D constraints, prescreen and primary-screen budgets, whether to enable the ML filter, whether to run another round), and test two things against the paper's hand-set 100K ATG+AL: **at equal docking budget, does the agent's configuration match or beat it**, and **does the agent stop by itself once returns saturate**? The reasons to pick this milestone: metric, baseline, reference upper bound and all code and data needed are open (GPL-2.0 code + the library on AWS Open Data + ten benchmark targets); the scale fits a single machine or small cluster (no 5.6 million vCPUs needed); and the outputs (agent traces plus a configuration schema) carry straight over to swapping in cardiac targets, plugging in a DrugCLIP-style scorer, or adding co-folding rescoring.

**Division of labour with the other three readings**: EXSCALATE is the "**exhaustive + HPC**" answer to the same problem (1 trillion evaluations) and AdaptiveFlow the "**adaptive + cloud**" answer — together they bracket an agent's compute-allocation policy (buy compute vs buy information); DrugCLIP's contrastive scorer could replace or precede the docking inside the ATG prescreen, since those 12 million dockings are the pipeline's largest fixed cost and the step most worth approximating with a foundation model; pairwise competitive docking (AF3/Boltz co-folding) lands exactly in AdaptiveFlow's third-stage "higher-accuracy rescoring" slot and supplies a ranking signal beyond docking score — which matters especially for the limitation that the ML layer here only learns the scoring function. **Important caveat**: this paper contains no cardiac target (FSP1, PARP1 and ten benchmark proteins, in a mostly oncology context), so the heart-disease direction can borrow its **workflow and infrastructure** only and must define its own targets, pathways and ADME constraints; FSP1 is a regulator of ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation, with a role in cancer resistance mentioned here, but **the paper says nothing about the heart** and relevance must not be inferred from it.

## Follow-ups
- Supplementary Tables 4–6, 8, 9 — the full list of the ~40 docking programs and ~1,500 protocols with their "special features" (protein–protein, RNA, explicit solvent/metal parameterization, covalent), together with the single-calculation timings in Extended Data Fig. 9: exactly the lookup table an agent needs for protocol choice and cost estimation.
- Supplementary Table 7 and Extended Data Figs. 1, 5–7 — the exact identity and structural provenance of the ten benchmark targets, the per-dimension binning schemes and occupancy statistics, and the complete production-scale benchmark numbers; required reading to reproduce the first milestone (the agent's action space *is* those bins).
- The AFLP / AFVS / AFU configuration-file examples in the Supplementary Information and the AFU conceptual and UML workflows in Supplementary Figs. 8 and 14 — the artifact an agent must generate is the configuration file ("the config *is* the action"), so its schema should be read first.
- Gorgulla et al., *Nature* 580, 663–668 (2020), "An open-source drug discovery platform enables ultra-large virtual screens" — the same corresponding author's previous-generation platform (Bash, 160,000 vCPUs, the 1.3-billion-molecule KEAP1–NRF2 screen), for quantifying what this generation actually saves on the same problem and what is genuinely new.
- Nigam et al., Tartarus (NeurIPS 2024) and "Drug discovery in low data regimes" (bioRxiv `10.1101/2023.10.03.560722`) — the same authors' work on inverse-molecular-design benchmarking and low-data pipelines, a ready-made evaluation context for using docking scores as objective functions in generative/optimization models.
- PoseBusters (Buttenschoen et al., *Chem. Sci.* 15, 3130–3139, 2024) and the original DiffDock / TANKBind / EquiBind papers integrated here — the former is the basis for an agent's automated sanity check (worth knowing what it catches and misses), the latter decide whether learned models can really replace physics-based docking in the prescreen layer.
