# AI-guided competitive docking for virtual screening and compound efficacy prediction

> **Bibkey** `Mirgaux_2026` · **Venue** npj Drug Discovery (2026) · **Category** drug-discovery · **Relevance** medium · **Access** open
> **Link** <https://doi.org/10.1038/s44386-026-00039-4> · `status: complete`

---

## One-liner
The authors note that diffusion-based co-folding models such as AF3 and Boltz-1/2 **produce a plausible-looking binding pose for any molecule**, and propose two plain but effective criteria: **(a) pose convergence** — the mean RMSD among poses predicted for the same ligand across several random seeds, where true inhibitors typically fall below 2 Å and within 5 Å of the pocket while unrelated molecules diverge and sit further away; and **(b) pairwise competitive docking** — co-fold two ligands at once and let whichever occupies the active site win, with the win rate as the **CDS (Competitive Docking Score)** used to rank inhibitors. Across 17 protein systems (19 binding sites), CDS rankings agree with experimental activity at concordance indices from 0.52 (no correlation) to 0.89 (strong), broadly on par with Boltz-2's own affinity-prediction module. Extended to "All-at-Once" (25–26 molecules competing in one run), screening 3,155 FDA-approved drugs for fluoroquinolones reached 93.5% enrichment with an enrichment factor of 62 after pose filtering.

## Problem
Diffusion-based generative models (RoseTTAFold All-Atom, AlphaFold3, Boltz-1/2) already predict protein–ligand complexes accurately, often beating classical docking programs, but carry a fundamental flaw: **they produce a binding pose for true binders and inactives alike**. Many models supply scoring metrics, yet inferring binding affinity accurately remains hard. The paper reduces this to two questions: (i) how can genuine binding be told apart from false positives? (ii) how can predicted poses be ranked so the order reflects expected binding affinity? These two questions motivate the whole study.

## Method
**(1) Pose criteria**: each ligand is inferred with **at least five random seeds**, each inference generating **at least 25 models**; predicted proteins are superimposed on the reference crystal structure (PyMOL's `super`), then two quantities are read off — the ligand's distance from the reference binding-site centre, and the mean RMSD among the predicted poses (pose convergence). The empirical thresholds for true inhibitors are distance < 5.0 Å and convergence < 2.0 Å. **(2) Pairwise competitive docking**: the protein model is given to the co-folding model together with **two competing ligands**, and whichever occupies the active site wins that run; each ligand pair gets at least n independent runs, and the win rate as a percentage is the CDS. All pairwise outcomes are compiled into a **pairwise matrix** and ligands ranked by cumulative CDS. **(3) All-at-Once (a cheaper simplification)**: instead of pairwise duels, a whole inhibitor class competes within a single inference. For virtual screening the 3,155-compound library was randomly split into **124 sets** (109 of 25 compounds, 15 of 26 — the set size dictated by GPU memory), one All-at-Once run each. **(4) Handling multi-site systems**: where a protein has several pockets, the other sites can be **blocked with known ligands** before letting the target ligand compete — which is how the paper forced allosteric inhibitors back to their correct site on DNA gyrase (gepotidacin blocking the NC site, sparfloxacin the FQ site). **(5) De novo design**: the **STONED algorithm** generated several thousand molecules in the chemical space around the top five FQs; 414 were selected and each competitively docked against the top-ranked FQ (STF). **(6) Models and compute**: the AF3 source was retrieved from GitHub on 14 Nov 2024 with parameters obtained directly from Google; Boltz-1/2 were installed locally from their official GitHub, including the steering versions that apply physics-based potentials. Inference ran on NVIDIA RTX 4080 (16 GB), RTX 3090 (24 GB) or A40 (48 GB), with sequence and template searches on an 11th-gen 8-core Intel CPU with 128 GB RAM; to save time, multiple sequence alignments were pre-generated per system and non-protein components (DNA, ions, compounds) incorporated before inference. Structural similarity used Tanimoto coefficients on RDKit ECFP4 fingerprints, pose plausibility was checked with PoseBusters, and statistics used the c-index (rank concordance), Pearson r and a Spearman test.

## Data
**Benchmark proteins**: 16 functionally diverse targets plus the structurally more complex multi-site system Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) DNA gyrase — 17 systems and 19 binding sites in total. Named targets include the kinases CDK2 and TYK2, the protease thrombin, the hydrolases PDE2 and BACE1, the kinesin KIF11/EG5, the phosphatases PTN11 and PTP1B, the oxidoreductases DHFR and COX-1/COX-2, the BCL-2-like protein MCL1, the GPCRs HCAR3 and FFA2R, the lectin GAL3, the PAS-domain EPAS1, and two later additions whose ligand-bound cryo-EM structures were only solved recently, GPER1 and SLC6A3. **Control molecules**: each benchmark target gets **28 unrelated "off-target" compounds** — one inhibitor from each of the other targets plus compounds binding proteins entirely unrelated to those studied. **DNA gyrase's three sites**: the FQ site (fluoroquinolones), the NC (non-catalytic) site where NBTIs bind, ~10 Å from the FQ site and between the two scissile DNA bonds, and the allosteric site of thiophene-class inhibitors, ~30 Å from the FQ site. **Experimental data for ranking**: from the literature and the Binding Database; in addition the authors **measured themselves** the IC50 values of 22 FQs inhibiting E. coli growth. **Screening library**: 3,155 FDA-approved molecules, of which 46 are FQs (1.5%). **Resistance variants**: four known DNA gyrase FQ-resistance variants that raise IC50 or MIC several-fold.

## Key results
- **Pose convergence separates true from false binders**: across the 16 benchmarks true inhibitors generally bind within ~5 Å of the pocket with convergence typically below 2 Å, while off-target molecules sit further away and vary far more. **Pose convergence generally outperformed Boltz-2's binding-likelihood prediction.** Specificity was strongest for CDK2, TYK2, EPAS1, PDE2, PTP1B, PTN11, DHFR, GAL3 and MCL1; weakest for the GPCRs FFA2R and HCAR3 and the hydrolase BACE1, then COX-1 — systems where some off-target molecules still landed near the pocket with low RMSD.
- **A multi-site system (DNA gyrase)**: FQs clustered within a mean 2 Å of their known site, as did the non-FQ spiropyrimidinetriones (QPT-1, zoliflodacin), which act at the FQ site anyway; NBTIs sat consistently within 2 Å of their site — **with AF3 and Boltz-1 but not Boltz-2**. The thiophene-class **allosteric** inhibitors, however, were rarely docked at their correct site by any of the models, being mislocalized to the FQ or NC sites — **unless those two sites were first blocked by known ligands**, after which they stayed put. The paper notes this has also been reported on an independent dataset of 20 orthosteric/allosteric ligand pairs across 17 proteins.
- **CDS rankings vs experimental activity (AF3)**: strong agreement for GAL3 (c = 0.89), TYK2 (0.87), thrombin (0.86), CDK2 (0.78), HCAR3 (0.77), KIF11 (0.76), the DNA gyrase allosteric site (0.76), PTN11 (0.75), PTP1B (0.74) and the DNA gyrase FQ site (0.72); moderate for FFA2R (0.66), EPAS1 (0.67), MCL1 (0.68), PDE2 (0.67), DHFR (0.68), the DNA gyrase NC site (0.62), BACE1 (0.62) and COX-2 (0.67); **COX-1 only 0.52, i.e. no correlation**. Notably the two worst systems (COX-1/COX-2) also had poor pose convergence, so the failure lies in the ligands never being reliably placed in the right pocket rather than in the competition mechanism. Substituting Boltz-1/2 for AF3 generally weakened correlations, with **no significant correlation** for COX-1, COX-2, PDE2, PTP1B and TYK2.
- **Versus Boltz-2's affinity module**: comparable overall across the 19 systems with only minor differences — Boltz-2 slightly better on BACE1, DHFR, EPAS1, FFA2R, PTP1B and PDE2; competitive docking slightly better on the DNA gyrase allosteric site, CDK2, GAL3, HCAR3, PTN11, TYK2 and COX-2. **Where both performed poorly (the DNA gyrase NC site, COX-1, COX-2) competitive docking still came out ahead** — the basis of its value as a fallback when direct AI affinity predictors fail.
- **Highly homologous pockets are indistinguishable**: CDS rankings for COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitors were nearly identical (Pearson r = 0.96), so AF3 cannot reliably separate the two cyclooxygenase isoenzymes, most likely because their binding sites are highly conserved. Likewise the four FQ-resistance variants shifted pose specificity and FQ rankings only **marginally** versus wild type — substituting one or two catalytic-site residues is not enough to change AF3's binding predictions, consistent with another study showing co-folding methods cannot account for intentional perturbations. In E. coli, switching to the secondary target topoisomerase IV did not refine rankings either, giving nearly the same order as DNA gyrase (r = 0.99).
- **Validation against their own measurements**: the 22 FQs' E. coli growth-inhibition IC50 values correlated modestly with CDS rankings overall but with a clear trend — lower-IC50 FQs generally rank higher; excluding two FQs predicted to be poorly soluble improved the correlation significantly (FQs are known for limited aqueous solubility).
- **All-at-Once for virtual screening**: on the 3,155 FDA-approved library (46 FQs, 1.5%), **147** top-ranking compounds were selected, **38 of them FQs** — a **25.9% enrichment**; the eight FQs not selected are known to have low activity against Mtb DNA gyrase and mostly belong to the first generation. Adding pose filtering (convergence 2.5 Å, distance 2.0 Å) raised FQ enrichment to **77.8%** among the 45 remaining compounds, and tightening both thresholds to 1.0 Å to **93.5%, with an enrichment factor of 62**. This closely matched Boltz-2's hit-identification performance on the same library. The computational point: instead of docking all 3,115 compounds individually in non-competitive mode, only the 147 selected by All-at-Once needed further analysis.
- **All-at-Once is coarser than pairwise**: competing a whole inhibitor class at once gave less detailed results — several potent inhibitors did not appear among the leaders, and too few compounds effectively occupied the site for reliable comparative analysis. It is good at separating strong FQs from weak FQs and non-FQs, not at fine-grained ranking.
- **De novo design (proof of concept)**: of 414 STONED-generated molecules, **31** occupied the FQ site in at least 70% of 100 generated models — i.e. predicted to bind more strongly than the current best, STF — with Tanimoto similarity to STF between 0.25 and 0.88. Since none appear in the CAS database (so they have likely never been synthesized), the authors filtered by predicted ADME properties, leaving only **8** with favourable drug-likeness, solubility and synthetic accessibility. These remain unsynthesized and experimentally unvalidated.

## Contributions
- Uses **pose convergence across seeds** as the criterion separating true from false binders, more reliable than the models' own binding-likelihood — a near-free signal obtained just by running more seeds.
- Turns **letting two ligands compete for the same site** into a ranking method: the authors consider simulating virtual competition between ligands with an ML tool a novel strategy (earlier competitive docking mostly placed multiple ligands side by side in large pockets, or served fragment-based design). It is interpretable — the winner is whoever occupies the site — and needs no external scoring function.
- **The site-blocking trick**: in multi-site systems, occupying the other pockets with known ligands forces allosteric inhibitors back to their correct site — a directly reusable practical move.
- All-at-Once gives a **two-step screening** cost structure: one multi-ligand competition cuts 3,155 compounds to 147, then pose analysis runs on those 147 alone, ending at 93.5% enrichment.
- Honestly reports the method's **system dependence** as part of the conclusion (c from 0.52 to 0.89) rather than only an average.

## Limitations
- **Not generalizable; must be assessed case by case.** The authors make this a conclusion: performance depends strongly on the protein system, inhibitor class, and molecular size and complexity. The root cause is that co-folding models' protein–ligand predictions are **heavily influenced by pose memorization from training data**, so performance is expected to be poor for ligands or binding environments absent from the training set. They tested this directly on two GPCR systems whose ligand-bound structures were solved recently (GPER1, SLC6A3): correlations were weak for both, while Boltz-2's IC50 predictions did better (c ≈ 0.6–0.7).
- **Large ligands break it.** Deep-learning co-folding models are prone to non-physical artefacts (steric clashes, stretched bonds, chirality violations), so the method tends to fail for large or complex ligands: two additional systems, HIV-2 protease (HIV2P) and tankyrase 2 (TNKS2), **could not be reliably evaluated** because the large competing inhibitors clashed extensively.
- **Ligand–ligand interaction is a confound**: in large pockets that can hold both competitors, or in multi-site systems like DNA gyrase, the outcome may be decided by ligand–ligand rather than ligand–protein interactions. A concrete case from the paper: when two FQs were docked together only one occupied the catalytic FQ site while the other often stacked against the DNA at the second catalytic site — which is only partially represented in the model used.
- **Cost scales quadratically**: building a full pairwise competitive matrix is O(n²) inferences, directly limiting how many ligands can be ranked; All-at-Once is cheaper but clearly coarser, and its set size is capped by GPU memory (25–26 compounds).
- **Cannot separate highly conserved homologous pockets** (COX-1 vs COX-2, r = 0.96) and **cannot sense resistance mutations** (changing 1–2 catalytic-site residues barely alters predictions) — both fatal for the practical needs of target selectivity and resistance prediction.
- **The de novo design is only a proof of concept**: all eight candidates are unsynthesized and unvalidated, which the authors explicitly leave to future work. Using AF3 also depends on obtaining model parameters from Google, an external dependency for reproducibility.
- **The comparison against Boltz-2 rests on too few systems**: the authors say a more robust comparison needs more of them. The steering Boltz versions removed atom clashes without significantly improving competitive-docking performance, and other models — RoseTTAFold All-Atom, Chai, Protenix, NeuralPlexer — remain unevaluated.

## Relation to our direction
This is one of the four papers Sikander assigned for **"Autonomous scientific orchestration of virtual screening"** (a Hayat Lab Master's/HiWi topic: LLM agents + molecular foundation models + docking + active learning, autonomously planning, executing and improving screening campaigns from protein structures, applied to heart disease). Its place in the pipeline is the **final re-ranking and truth-checking layer**: `drugclip-science-2026` retrieves candidates from hundreds of millions in seconds → ATG-VS from `adaptiveflow-natbiotech-2026` docks at scale → this paper's AF3 competitive docking ranks the last few dozen and discards false positives. **(1) The two most useful things for an agent**: **pose convergence** is a nearly free **self-consistency check** — run more seeds, see whether the predictions agree — exactly the signal an agent lacks when judging whether to trust a tool's output, and in this paper it **beats the model's own binding-likelihood**; and **the CDS win rate** is naturally bounded, interpretable and accumulable, better suited as an agent's decision variable than a raw score. **(2) The decisions for an agent**: when to escalate from cheap retrieval/docking to expensive co-folding (the paper's two-step method already provides the cost structure: 3,155 → 147 → pose analysis); whether to rank by pairwise duels (expensive, fine-grained) or All-at-Once (cheap, coarse); whether to block sites first in a multi-site system, and with which known ligand; how tight to set pose filters (the paper gives 2.5 Å/2.0 Å and 1.0 Å, with enrichment from 77.8% to 93.5% but fewer surviving compounds — a precision/recall trade-off that is exactly the agent's accounting). **(3) A sensible first milestone**: **build this layer as a verifier rather than a ranker.** Reproduce the two-step All-at-Once screen using the paper's public Zenodo data and the 3,155-compound FDA library, wrap "multi-seed pose convergence" as a standalone tool, then attach it after any upstream layer (DrugCLIP's top-k or ATG-VS's top hits) and quantify how many false positives it removes. Score it by enrichment factor against the known FQ labels as ground truth — **no wet lab needed at all**. **(4) This paper matters most for the agent-trustworthiness line**: it supplies an **external reliability criterion independent of the model's own score** (pose convergence plus competition win rate), and explicitly shows the model's self-assessment (Boltz-2 binding likelihood) is worse than that external criterion — the same argument we make for replacing model self-scoring with an external verifier, here landed at the molecular level. **(5) Pitfalls to watch**: (a) **memorization, not physics** — co-folding performance rests on similar poses in the training set, so an agent will systematically overestimate itself on novel scaffolds or new pockets, with **no** calibrated uncertainty available; the GPER1/SLC6A3 failures are the warning; (b) **homologous pockets are indistinguishable**, so this layer **cannot be used for selectivity judgements** (which matters, since cardiac targets have many same-family members); (c) **resistance mutations are invisible to it**, so do not expect resistance prediction; (d) the O(n²) cost must be explicitly budgeted by the agent rather than left to free-running pairwise duels; (e) large ligands fail outright through clashes, so the agent needs an upfront molecular size/complexity check to decide whether this route is viable at all.

- **Borrowable designs:** multi-seed pose convergence (distance < 5.0 Å, RMSD < 2.0 Å) as a true/false-binder criterion; pairwise competition → CDS win-rate matrix → cumulative ranking; the All-at-Once two-step cost structure; the site-blocking trick for multi-site systems; building a negative-control set from 28 off-target molecules; and using the c-index rather than regression error as the headline metric, because ordering compounds right matters more than predicting exact values.
- **Generative tool:** the STONED algorithm for de novo generation in the chemical space around known actives.
