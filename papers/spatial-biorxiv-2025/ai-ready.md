# Mapping and reprogramming human tissue microenvironments with MintFlow

## Abstract
Abstract
                
                  Tissue microenvironments reprogram local cellular states in disease, yet current computational spatial methods remain descriptive and do not simulate tissue perturbation. We present MintFlow, a generative AI algorithm that learns how the tissue microenvironment influences cell states and predicts how tissue perturbations can reprogram them. Applied to three human diseases, MintFlow uncovered distinct pathogenic spatial reprogramming in inflammatory and tumor microenvironments. In atopic dermatitis, MintFlow identified a novel, spatially-imprinted, type 2 (
                  IL13
                  +
                  ITGAE
                  +
                  ) epidermal T resident memory cell population (type 2 T
                  RM
                  ), and decoded signaling pathways within the perivascular lymphoid niche. In melanoma, MintFlow identified fibrotic stroma resembling keloid scar tissue. In kidney cancer, MintFlow resolved immunosuppressed CD8
                  +
                  T cell states within tertiary lymphoid structures. Furthermore, MintFlow enabled
                  in silico
                  perturbations of disease-relevant cell states and tissue environments. Regulatory T cell modulation in atopic dermatitis was predicted to suppress the pro-inflammatory tissue environment, supporting manipulation of these cells as a therapeutic target. In kidney cancer, in silico T cell replacement recapitulated immune checkpoint blockade, while spatially targeted macrophage depletion reverted immunosuppressed T cell states. The corresponding gene programs correlated with survival in large kidney cancer patient cohorts. Together, these findings position MintFlow as a tool for unbiased disease mechanism prediction and in silico perturbation, accelerating translational hypothesis generation and guiding therapeutic strategies.

### Introduction
Recent advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics enable dissection of tissue architecture and cell identity at high resolution, mapping how cell types, transcriptional programs, and cell-cell interactions contribute to health and disease in native spatial contexts. Pathological outcomes often arise not only from changes within individual cells, but from disrupted interactions between cells and their microenvironment (e.g. spatially restricted immunosuppressive networks driving immune evasion in tumors, chemokine gradients sustaining chronic inflammation in atopic dermatitis, glial-neuronal signaling in neuroinflammation). A key challenge is to distinguish intrinsic gene programs that define cellular identity from effects induced by the surrounding microenvironment. Existing methods either fail to disentangle intrinsic vs microenvironment-induced variation, lack mechanistic interpretability (require post-hoc embedding interpretation), rely on linear assumptions, do not scale, or require prior knowledge of gene-gene / ligand-receptor interactions. They also fall short at prioritizing microenvironments/genes for hypotheses and at enabling in silico perturbation.

The authors present MintFlow, a generative AI framework based on flow matching with a theoretically grounded (identifiability-proven) solution that disentangles intrinsic from microenvironment-induced gene expression in spatial transcriptomics. Without relying on prior knowledge, predefined domains, or ligand-receptor pairs, MintFlow learns microenvironment-induced gene programs (MGP) at single-cell resolution and enables in silico perturbation of the tissue microenvironment, simulating how changes in local cellular context alter gene expression. Applied across atopic dermatitis, melanoma, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), MintFlow uncovered spatially organized, disease-specific cell states. The authors envision MintFlow as a step toward AI-driven construction of virtual tissue models.

### MintFlow overview (Fig. 1)
Given tissue sections profiled with single-cell-resolution spatial transcriptomics, each cell's microenvironment is determined from spatial coordinates. Cell type labels and each cell's microenvironment cell type composition (MCC) serve as supervision. MintFlow infers three embedding vectors per cell: intrinsic signals (z_n), incoming spatial signals from the microenvironment (s_in), and outgoing spatial signals to the microenvironment (s_out); s_out signals of a cell's neighbors are aggregated. Using flow matching, z_n and s_in are transformed into intrinsic and microenvironment-induced embeddings, decoded to generate intrinsic (X_int) and microenvironment-induced (X_mic) read counts, which sum to reconstruct original read counts (X = X_int + X_mic). Clustering the microenvironment-induced embeddings yields fine-grained microenvironment-induced cell states (MCS). A "microenvironment score" quantifies the relative influence of microenvironment signals on each cell, highlighting spatial signaling hotspots. Users derive MGPs (via standard tools like scanpy), compare conditions, stratify patients, and simulate perturbing microenvironments to study effects on MCS and MGPs.

### Benchmarking
On simulated data with known microenvironment-induced effects as ground truth, MintFlow significantly outperformed alternative methods and random baselines at predicting microenvironment-induced gene expression across three metrics (MAE, EMD, MSE; metrics negated so higher is better). On real data (single-sample Xenium of atopic dermatitis and melanoma, and a multi-sample Xenium dataset of ten atopic dermatitis sections), MintFlow assigned a higher proportion of read counts of known signaling genes to the microenvironment-induced component than alternatives, which either over-assigned signaling genes to intrinsic expression or minimized microenvironment contribution. Only MintFlow was scalable enough to be applied to all 10 skin eczema tissue sections.

### Atopic dermatitis: microenvironment-induced T_RM cell state and T cell activation hub
Applied to 10 human skin tissue sections from 8 individuals with atopic dermatitis, profiled by Xenium 5k (8 newly generated sections), including inflamed (lesional) and non-lesional skin; after QC, 197,487 cells. MintFlow found high microenvironment scores in the superficial dermis, some correlating with perivascular inflammatory infiltrate on H&E. Clustering microenvironment-induced expression identified 10 distinct microenvironment domains. One cluster (T_DC), prominent in superficial dermis, was predominantly T cells with antigen-presenting cells (MigDCs, cDC2), and was expanded in inflamed vs non-inflamed skin.

The T_DC MGP included T cell activation/homing genes (ITGB2, SELPLG), T cell-APC interaction genes (ICAM3, ITGAL/LFA-1), type 2 polarization genes (IL21R, ALOX15), and chemotactic genes (CCL19, CCL22). Cell-cell communication analysis highlighted a CCL19/CCR7 axis (FRC-like and perivascular fibroblasts recruiting MigDCs and T cells) and a CCL22/CCL17-CCR4 axis (MigDC-T interactions). Pathway analysis: most enriched process was T cell activation, suggesting T_DC is a T cell activation hub. drug2cell analysis correctly identified known atopic dermatitis drugs (upadacitinib, a JAK inhibitor; prednisolone) plus candidates (Amlexanox, a PDE4 inhibitor; pseudoephedrine). The T_DC MGP was validated in external scRNA-seq (higher in inflamed skin of 4 patients) and across a 23-disease / 6-tissue atlas, scoring highest in eosinophilic CRSwNP (a type 2 immunity disease responsive, like atopic dermatitis, to dupilumab), and high in IBD and psoriatic arthritis.

Disentangling T cell expression: the intrinsic latent space showed naive (CCR7, SELL) and regulatory (FOXP3, TIGIT, CTLA4) clusters; the microenvironment-induced space revealed a distinct CD8A+ ITGAE+ GZMB+ T cell cluster with increased IL13, IL22, GZMB located in superficial skin — characteristic of epidermal T_RM cells with a spatially-imprinted phenotype (ITGAE and GZMB were reported spatially imprinted in mouse intestinal CD8+ T cells), a novel microenvironment-induced pathogenic T cell state in atopic dermatitis.

### In silico spatial perturbations model cellular therapy in atopic dermatitis (Fig. 3)
Two in silico perturbations in the same inflamed skin region: (1) Treg depletion (remove Tregs from microenvironment); (2) Treg augmentation (replace all T conv with Tregs). Under Treg depletion, MigDCs upregulated genes for inflammatory response (rank 1), MAPK cascade regulation (rank 3), T cell activation (rank 4), lymphocyte cell-cell adhesion (rank 6), myeloid-leukocyte differentiation (rank 10); top DEGs included chemokines CCL17, CCL22. T conv upregulated T cell activation (rank 1), inflammatory response (rank 2), IL-2 signaling (rank 3) — an increased inflammatory / worsened phenotype, consistent with IPEX syndrome. Under Treg augmentation, inflammatory pathways were NOT enriched in MigDCs; top processes related to cytoskeleton, cell size, stress response. Migration/maturation markers (CCR7, LAMP3, CD274, EBI3), chemokines (CCL17, CCL22), and type 2 genes (MMP12, ALOX15) increased under depletion and decreased under augmentation. In vivo validation: 10 atopic dermatitis patients 12 weeks post-dupilumab; in responders (>=50% improvement) Tregs typically increased from baseline to week 12, while 2 non-responders (<=10% improvement) showed no significant change and elevated baseline Tregs. Supports Treg modulation as a potential therapeutic strategy.

### Melanoma: inducible keloid-like stroma (Fig. 4)
Applied to a public human cutaneous melanoma dataset (5000-plex Xenium); after QC, 98,749 cells. Highest microenvironment scores were outside tumor nests. 8 microenvironment clusters, including two tumor-predominant clusters (Melanoma1, Melanoma2) occupying spatially distinct regions. Both expressed melanoma markers (MLANA, PRAME), but Melanoma1 MGP uniquely contained EDNRB, CST1, BAALC, GDF15. Pathway analysis: Melanoma1 enriched for amino acid transport and glycogen catabolism; Melanoma2 for cell migration and microtubule polymerization. The Stroma domain (CAFs and T cells) MGP included CXCL12 and ECM proteins; CXCL12 attracts CXCR4+ immune cells, and an inducible CXCL12-CXCR4 axis excludes T cells from tumor nests. ECM genes encoded collagens (COL4A2, COL5A1, COL5A2, COL11A1) and cross-linking enzymes (LOX, LOXL1, LOXL2), forming a physical barrier. Scoring the Stroma MGP across fibroblasts from 23 skin diseases: highest in cutaneous melanoma and also high in keloid scarring (a fibroproliferative dense stroma with collagen cross-linking) — an inducible keloid-like, immune-excluding stroma.

### Kidney cancer (ccRCC): tertiary lymphoid structure with distinct T cell states (Fig. 5)
Applied to ccRCC from a patient: three tumor core regions plus the tumor-normal interface profiled by 5000-plex Xenium; after integration/annotation, 337,116 cells across 24 cell types (successful batch integration). Microenvironment score varied regionally; a prominent signaling hotspot appeared in Core 2. Histology showed a dense lymphocytic aggregate; a TLS gene signature score (CXCL13, CCR7, LTA, CXCR5, CD8A, CD4, CCL19, CD79A, MS4A1, CD3E) confirmed a tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS). Subsetting MintFlow T cell embeddings revealed six microenvironment-induced T cell states, three localized within the TLS: TLS Core 1, TLS Core 2, TLS Border. These states were not recoverable from intrinsic embeddings or fine-grained reclustering of the original data. Tumor-infiltrating T cells enriched for exhaustion markers (HAVCR2, TOX, LAG3), highest in TLS Border cells; CXCR3 enriched in TLS subsets with ligands CXCL9/CXCL10 spatially enriched in the TLS. Similar TLS T cell states were found across additional ccRCC patients.

### Inhibitory macrophage-T cell crosstalk within TLS (Fig. 6)
Reanalyzed a published ccRCC scRNA-seq dataset (147,917 tumor-infiltrating cells, 10 patients, including the spatially-profiled donor). TLS T cell programs were detectable across low/intermediate/high Leibovich risk groups but not spatially demarcated in scRNA-seq alone. LIANA ligand-receptor analysis showed TLS T cells had the highest predicted interactions with myeloid populations, especially macrophages; top pairs enriched for MHC class I/II and co-inhibitory signals (LAG3, HAVCR2). Independent in vivo validation used Kaede transgenic mice with subcutaneous colorectal tumor cells treated with anti-PD-L1 vs isotype; the TLS Border program (not Core 1/2) was selectively enriched in memory and exhausted CD8+ T cells (exhausted almost exclusively photoconverted/tumor-resident), and anti-PD-L1 upregulated Il2rb, Stat1, Ltb in TLS Border-high exhausted cells. In the human Xenium data, subclustering MintFlow macrophage embeddings resolved a TLS-localized tumor-associated macrophage subset with high TREM2 and CCR1, enriched for antigen presentation, interferon response, and T cell co-inhibition. Vasculature and fibroblasts showed no distinct TLS-localized states, implicating macrophage-T cell interactions as the primary immune suppressors.

### In silico perturbations predict therapeutic potential of TLS macrophage targeting (Fig. 7)
Validity check: curated an ICB-exposed T cell signature from published ccRCC scRNA-seq (pre/post immune checkpoint blockade); virtually replaced immunosuppressed TLS-resident T cells with post-ICB-like T cells. Of macrophage genes known to be upregulated post-ICB, 12 were on the Xenium 5K panel and expressed in >10% of macrophages; 11 of 12 were upregulated upon virtual replacement (including CXCL9, CXCL10). Then, in silico deletion of TLS-associated macrophages reprogrammed TLS T cells: downregulated co-inhibition/exhaustion markers (PD-L2, TIM3, VSIR, CD276, LILRB1, LAIR1) and upregulated TCR signaling molecules (PLCG1, STIM1, TRAF6) and interleukin receptors (IL4R, IL6ST, IL15RA). Clinical significance in TCGA (606 ccRCC tumors, bulk RNA-seq): TLS Core 1/2 and TLS macrophage programs were not significantly associated with survival, but high TLS Border T cell program (pre-perturbation) was associated with worse overall survival (p = 0.0073), while the reprogrammed (post-macrophage-removal) program was associated with a survival advantage (p = 0.0034). Several MintFlow-identified genes are translationally relevant: LAIR1 (upregulated in RCC, poor survival), TIM3 (associated with immunotherapy response), LILRB1 inhibitors (early-phase kidney cancer trials).

### Discussion / Limitations
MintFlow recovers microenvironment-induced transcriptional programs obscured in traditional analyses, recapitulates established features, and uncovers novel clinically relevant cell states across inflammatory and malignant contexts, plus enables in silico perturbation for virtual tissue reprogramming. Many perturbations (cell type-specific depletion within intact tissue) are infeasible at scale in vitro/in vivo. Limitations: sensitivity to low read counts; needs consistent gene panels across sections; flow matching is computationally intensive during training (mitigated by a neighbor sampling strategy enabling training on multiple sections / millions of cells); susceptible to segmentation errors; released as open-source but training can be challenging and requires dataset-specific hyperparameter fine-tuning. Future: extend beyond multiple sections to multi-tissue spatial atlases and transfer learning.

### Methods (model)
The generative model explicitly models intrinsic and spatial signals between a cell and its microenvironment. Microenvironments are defined via a k-Nearest Neighbors graph (k=5); MCC is the proportion of each cell type among a cell's neighbors. s_out (outgoing signal, a priori conditioned on cell type) is aggregated across neighbors by simple neighborhood averaging into s_in (incoming signal), indirectly conditioning on MCC. z_n (a priori conditioned on cell type) captures intrinsic variation (receptors, cell cycle, housekeeping genes). Expression has two components: not-externally-triggered (encoded from z_n) and externally-triggered (encoded from s_in and z_n). Because training flow-based models on high-dimensional data is impractical, flow matching is performed in latent space using a neural ODE decoder to obtain low-dimensional intrinsic and microenvironment-induced components, then decoders generate X_int and X_mic with X = X_int + X_mic, adding interpretability (removes need for archetypal/linear-probe analysis).

Identifiability: nonlinear decoders are generally unidentifiable, but conditioning latent factors on side information (cell type CT and MCC), mimicking iVAE, makes [z, s_in] identifiable up to permutation. Additional objectives assign CT-related dimensions to z and MCC-related dimensions to s_in. To make the X = X_int + X_mic decomposition unique, optimal-transport constraints are imposed (Proposition 1: with a Euclidean-cost transport functional and the summation constraint, the decomposition is unique). Flow matching objectives keep the marginal vector field simple/near-optimal. Two further objectives (implemented via Wasserstein distribution matching with cell type-specific discriminators) enforce that intrinsic embeddings cannot predict neighboring cell types. Cells of the same type are encouraged to have close intrinsic embeddings via an MSE loss.

Scalability: representations are interleaved via the spatial graph (s_out averaging in the generative model; a GNN decomposing x_n given neighbors on the inference side). Inductive graph learning with subsampled subgraphs (central nodes with full k-hop neighborhoods, non-central context nodes) enables training on subgraphs rather than the full graph, using PyTorch Geometric's neighbor loader.
