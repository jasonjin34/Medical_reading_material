# A cross-species single-cell kidney epigenome atlas reveals epithelial-dominant aging-like states in disease

- **Authors:** Jeong, Hyeonsoo; Lake, Blue B.; Diep, Dinh; Li, Xuwen; Yan, Qi; Gisch, Debora L.; Kaushal, Madhurima; Reinert, Stephanie; Eadon, Michael T.; Gaut, Joseph P.; Jain, Sanjay; Zhang, Kun
- **Venue / Year:** Nature Aging · 2026
- **DOI:** 10.1038/s43587-026-01221-z
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-026-01221-z
- **Bibkey:** Jeong_2026
- **Status:** complete

## Abstract
Epigenetic aging is a hallmark of chronic diseases. While such epigenetic changes can arise following tissue injury, the cell types most affected remain largely unknown. Here we built a cross-species single-cell multiomics atlas of DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility and transcription profiles from healthy, injured (human) and aged (mouse) kidneys. We found that tubular epithelial cells in diseased kidneys exhibit pronounced accelerated epigenetic aging and showed that this pathological state mirrors transcriptional trajectories observed during aging, driven by preferential dysregulation of lineage-specific genes lacking CpG islands. Spatially, these epigenetic changes mapped to pathological niches of unresolved repair. Co-profiling single-cell DNA methylation and 3D genome architecture revealed that epithelial repair states in disease undergo significant higher-order genome reorganizations, alongside activation of genes associated with renal decline. Together, our findings characterize a loss of epigenetic repression within coordinated three-dimensional chromatin structures and reduced local methylome integrity, which compromises epithelial cell identity during aging and impedes repair.

## Main
Chronic diseases, such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), represent a major public health challenge, placing an immense burden on patients and healthcare systems worldwide1,2. CKD often stems from progressive decline in kidney function due to prolonged injury and inflammation, leading to impaired functional repair of nephron cell types. Instead of resolving, some injured cells can adopt a persistent, dysfunctional state that contributes to fibrosis and organ failure3,4,5,6. Epigenetic mechanisms are essential for establishing and maintaining cell identity and modulating cellular responses to stress. While these mechanisms normally guide successful repair, epigenetic dysregulation can shift tissues from adaptive repair to a persistent pathological state7,8; however, with multiple cell types present in the kidney, the cell-type-specific epigenetic programs associated with this failure in chronic diseases are not yet well understood.
Dissecting these mechanisms of kidney disease pathogenesis has been challenging due to the complex cellular heterogeneity of the kidney. While recent single-cell studies have provided insights into transcriptomes and chromatin accessibilities5,9,10,11, a cell type-resolved view of the deeper epigenetic layers governing cell identity remains missing. Specifically, DNA methylation provides a stable, long-term record of cellular states12,13, while three-dimensional (3D) genome architecture governs the physical topology required for gene regulation14,15. Capturing these layers is essential to understand how regulatory circuits are rewired in chronic conditions. Furthermore, while aging is known to be a primary risk factor for CKD16, the interplay between normal age-related epigenetic drift and the specific alterations driving disease pathology remains to be elucidated.
To address these gaps, we built a cross-species, multimodal single-cell kidney epigenome atlas spanning healthy and diseased human kidneys and young and aged mouse kidneys. This cross-species dataset enabled us to examine conserved and divergent methylation features associated with age and disease. By generating single-cell DNA methylomes together with matched single-cell RNA and chromatin accessibility profiles, we identified the cell-type-specific epigenetic changes and found that disease-associated DNA methylation clock age acceleration was most pronounced in tubular epithelia. Our atlas uniquely integrates these modalities with spatial transcriptomics (Xenium) and single-cell methyl-Hi-C (scMethyl-Hi-C17) to map these changes to specific tissue niches and revealed a potential higher-order chromatin reorganization (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our findings provide a key resource and insights into spatially anchored gene expression changes integrated with single-cell epigenetic changes across ages with cross-species validations.

### Results

### A multimodal epigenome atlas of the human and mouse kidneys
We applied a cellular combinatorial indexing method (sciMET18) to generate a cross-species single-cell DNA methylation atlas comprising 57,619 high-quality nuclei. This dataset spans 12 human donors (33,835 nuclei from seven patients with kidney disease and five age-matched reference tissues; Supplementary Table 1) and six mice (23,784 nuclei from three young and three aged animals; Supplementary Table 2), yielding a median of 0.91 million CpG sites (approximately 3.1% CpG coverage of the human genome) per cell. To minimize technical variation, human samples were pooled for processing in one batch and deconvoluted based on SNPs. Donor-specific variants from whole-genome DNA sequencing validated multiplexing accuracy, with 99.2% of sciMET nuclei assigned to the correct donor of origin.
To link these epigenetic profiles to gene regulation, tissue niches and chromatin architecture, we integrated the single-cell DNA methylation atlas with matched multimodal datasets. We generated single-cell multiomes for the matched human (142,459 nuclei from nine samples) and mouse samples (36,563 nuclei from six samples) to directly connect DNA methylation with chromatin accessibility and gene expression (Supplementary Table 1). We further contextualized these findings by generating spatial transcriptomics maps using Xenium for representative healthy, acute kidney injury (AKI) and CKD samples (383,771 cells from four samples), and defined the baseline 3D chromatin compartments using scMethyl-Hi-C on a healthy reference donor. This multilayered approach establishes a framework for characterizing cell-type-specific epigenetic features associated with age and disease.
We first sought to accurately identify and annotate kidney cell types based on their unique epigenetic profiles. Unsupervised clustering of human single-cell methylomes successfully identified all known major kidney cell types (Fig. 1a). We annotated these clusters based on promoter hypomethylation of canonical marker genes, such as PODXL for podocytes (POD) and SLC34A1 for proximal tubule (PT) cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). Assessment of orthologous genes ensured consistent identification of matched cell types in the mouse kidney. Global DNA methylation levels were largely stable, ranging from 69.1% to 81.7% across most lineages, with the exception of immune cells, which exhibited greater heterogeneity of CpG methylation consistent with their diverse functional states (Fig. 1b). Aggregating single-cell profiles yielded pseudobulk cell type methylomes ranging from 28.35 to 29.2 million CpGs, ensuring robust detection of regulatory elements. Validation against reference bulk methylomes19 confirmed the high accuracy of our cell type assignments, while our single-cell approach enabled distinction of subpopulations within the tubular epithelium at high resolution (Supplementary Fig. 3).
We investigated the relationship between chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation at cell-type-specific regulatory elements using the matched single-cell multiome data. While the cellular compositions from both modalities showed strong concordance (Spearman’s rho = 0.72; Fig. 1c), sciMET detected a higher proportion of immune cells, particularly myeloid lineages. This discrepancy may be attributed to the possibility that single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) under-detects immune populations, which can have lower nuclear RNA content. As expected, cell-type-specific ATAC peaks displayed clear hypomethylation in their corresponding cell type (Fig. 1d). To further characterize regulatory elements, we segmented the genome based on methylation patterns (Methods), identifying unmethylated regions (UMRs; <10% methylation, CpG rich) and low-methylated regions (LMRs; 10–50% methylation, CpG-poor). These regions are known to be characteristic of promoters and enhancers, respectively20. A majority of UMRs (92.6%) overlapped with ATAC peaks, which is consistent with the nature of open and active promoters (Fig. 1e). In contrast, a smaller proportion of LMRs (54.8%) overlapped with ATAC peaks, suggesting greater variability in the relationship between methylation and accessibility within distal regulatory elements. Collectively, this multimodal integration highlights the concordance between chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation at promoters, while capturing their distinct patterns at distal regulatory elements, establishing a rigorous high-resolution framework for characterizing kidney epigenomes.

### Epigenetic conservation and divergence in kidney cell types
To investigate whether genomic regions lacking evolutionary constraint are disproportionately vulnerable to age-related dysregulation, we jointly analyzed human and mouse single-cell methylomes to quantify evolutionary conservation. To address sparse measurements, we aggregated each cell with its 50 nearest neighbors into metacells (Methods). These metacells were then profiled across 40,284 one-to-one orthologous 5-kbp bins, and the resulting datasets were integrated using Harmony21. Following batch correction, human and mouse nuclei were co-clustered by cell type (Fig. 2a). Methylation patterns at canonical marker genes were consistent across homologous cell types. Leiden clustering in the integrated space identified 13 consensus kidney lineages. This was achieved using a reference panel of 2,180 conserved hypermethylated or hypomethylated regions shared between species (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 3). Overall, the cell type composition was largely similar between species, with abundance differences potentially due to differential sampling.
Analysis of differential methylation across human kidney cell types revealed 208,049 cell-type-specific differentially methylated regions (DMRs). Whole methylome sequencing, although sparse at each cell, proved advantageous, as only 16.4% of these human DMRs overlapped with CpGs on the Illumina 850K array, suggesting that previous studies based on methylation arrays missed a larger fraction of regulatory elements in the genome. Of the cell-type DMRs in human, 138,110 regions were mappable to the mouse genome, with 57,259 aligning to one-to-one orthologous loci across species (Fig. 2c). We observed substantial conservation of cell-type-specific methylation states among these orthologous DMRs, with 34% of human DMRs retaining the same state in mice; however, epithelial cell types displayed significantly greater inter-species methylation divergence compared to non-epithelial populations (Fig. 2d). This divergence was associated with lower underlying sequence conservation, which we observed particularly in DMRs from proximal and intermediate tubule epithelial cells (Fig. 2c). This observation aligns with previous cross-species studies showing greater species-specific divergence in epithelial populations compared to neural, muscle and immune lineages, likely reflecting faster epigenetic evolution at distal regulatory elements in epithelia22.
Analysis of differential methylation between species revealed distinct evolutionary patterns. Conserved cell type DMRs were enriched at transcription start sites and Polycomb repressed chromatin states, indicating active silencing of alternative lineage programs is an evolutionarily conserved mechanism for maintaining kidney cell identity across species. In contrast, species-specific DMRs were predominantly located in distal, lowly methylated enhancer and intergenic regions (Fig. 2e).
In PT cells, both conserved and nonconserved DMRs showed enrichment for HNF4A and PPARA transcription factor (TF) binding motifs, highlighting their consistent roles in PT identity across mammals; however, AP-1 and MYC family TF motifs were exclusively enriched in species-divergent PT DMRs, suggesting species-specific differences in the regulation of stress response, proliferation and metabolic pathways (Supplementary Table 4). Functional annotation using GREAT23 linked conserved PT DMRs to core nephron functions such as ‘organic anion transport,’ ‘urate transport,’ and ‘cellular amino acid metabolic processes’ (Supplementary Table 5). Conversely, the divergent PT DMRs were enriched for genome maintenance and stability processes closely associated with aging and cellular senescence, including ‘negative regulation of telomeric DNA binding,’ ‘negative regulation of histone H2A K63 linked ubiquitination’ and ‘protein repair.’ They also showed enrichment for terms related to broader developmental patterning, including neural development, indicating weaker evolutionary constraints in the kidney or context-specific roles in other tissues (Supplementary Table 5).
Collectively, these findings indicate that evolutionarily stable differential methylation governs core renal physiology, while divergence in enhancer elements is enriched for chromatin maintenance and stress response pathways. These characteristics may contribute to the increased susceptibility of human epithelial cells to age-related epigenetic drift and disease-associated remodeling.

### Age is associated with cell-type-specific epigenetic remodeling in the mouse kidney
Our analysis of the mouse kidney methylome captured a strong aging signature, as an epigenetic clock based on global DNA methylation accurately differentiated young (4 months) from aged (20 months) mice (Fig. 3a). We first characterized the methylation patterns of putative regulatory regions (UMRs and LMRs) across cell types. This revealed that regions with cell-type-specific hypomethylation were enriched in LMRs, whereas regions shared across cell types were enriched in UMRs (Fig. 3b,c). We then investigated whether aging selectively impacts these elements. Indeed, cell-type-specific UMR/LMRs exhibited significantly greater hypermethylation (Fig. 3d and Extended Data Fig. 1) and more pronounced age-related methylation shifts compared to their shared counterparts (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 2). This suggests that the regulatory elements maintaining specialized cell identities are intrinsically more susceptible to age-related decay than those governing core, shared cellular functions.
To understand how age-related methylation changes correlate with cell-type relatedness, we quantified cell-type-specific UMR and LMR methylation shifts in their unassociated cell types (Methods). A strong inverse relationship was observed between inter-cell-type methylation distance and the magnitude of aging-associated methylation change, indicating that regulatory regions defining closely related cell types were more vulnerable to age-associated epigenomic dysregulation (Fig. 3f and Extended Data Fig. 3). This pattern was exclusive to LMRs, suggesting that the epigenetic signatures of distal enhancers were more susceptible to age-related changes than those of more broadly active promoters. Additionally, regions that became dysregulated with age tended to be closer to regulatory elements, implying that the chromatin context may facilitate methylation remodeling at already accessible sites (Extended Data Fig. 4). These results suggest that regulatory elements potentially underlying distinct phenotypic functionalities of closely related cell types might be more susceptible to age-associated dysfunction.
Differential methylation analysis revealed that age-associated DMRs were significantly enriched in cell-type-specific cis-regulatory regions (Fig. 3g), suggesting these loci are preferential targets of age-related epigenetic drift. This enrichment reflects a progressive loss of epigenetic identity. Specifically, cell types gained methylation at regions that were hypomethylated in young mice and concurrently showed age-associated hypomethylation at cis-regulatory regions specific to other cell types. This divergence of epigenetic cell type identities was further evident at the PT subtype level, where hypomethylated DMRs in one segment were enriched in the LMRs of an adjacent PT segment (Fig. 3g).
Our findings indicate that the regulatory elements defining cell identity are preferentially affected by age-associated methylation changes. To examine whether these aging-associated signatures overlap with regulatory regions altered after kidney injury, we analyzed single-nucleus ATAC-seq data from a mouse ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) model defining resolved and unresolved PT repair states24. Age-associated DMRs were significantly enriched within injury-associated accessible regions (fold enrichment = 2.95, P < 0.01, permutation test; Fig. 3h). This overlap identifies regulatory loci that are responsive in both aging and injury contexts.

### Disease-associated DNA methylation clock age acceleration is most pronounced in tubular epithelial cells
To investigate whether kidney disease is associated with DNA methylation clock age acceleration, we compared single-cell methylomes from patients with kidney disease with those from age-matched healthy controls. Our initial analysis of sample-level pseudobulk methylomes revealed significant epigenetic age acceleration in the disease cohort (P = 0.01, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). We further ensured the robustness of this finding by checking for technical bias through downsampling reads and employing a principal component-trained version of the Horvath clock25, which performs robustly with low-coverage methylome data. Age predictions remained accurate at shallow coverage (down to approximately 3×), supporting the reliability of the epigenetic age calculation (Extended Data Fig. 5).
Cell-type level analysis showed that epigenetic age acceleration in kidney disease was dominated by epithelial cells (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 4). Epithelial cell pseudobulk methylomes from the disease cohort showed an average clock age acceleration of 4.8 years relative to healthy controls (P = 2.3 × 10−4), whereas non-epithelial populations did not show a significant difference (Fig. 4a). This epithelial-specific age acceleration seems to be due to cell-intrinsic epigenetic changes, rather than shifts in cell type composition, as disease samples showed similar epithelial proportions while showing increases in fibroblasts and immune cells (Fig. 4b).
To validate our findings, we used bulk whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data generated independently from laser-microdissected kidney tubulointerstitial (n = 15) and glomerular (n = 15) tissues26. We performed cell type deconvolution using our single-cell methylome atlas as a reference to examine cell type proportions within the bulk methylome data (Fig. 4c). This deconvolution approach identified an expected enrichment of tubular epithelial cells in tubulointerstitial samples and of POD, endothelial cells and parietal epithelial cells in glomerular samples (Fig. 4d). Epigenetic clock estimates from these deconvoluted cell populations confirmed that age acceleration in disease patients is most pronounced in tissues enriched for tubular epithelial cells (Fig. 4e).
Building upon our previous finding that altered PT states of unresolving or maladaptive repair are associated with chronic disease progression9,10, we sought to link the regulatory elements of kidney cell types to genetic kidney disease risk. Using stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (S-LDSC)27, we observed a strong enrichment of PT-specific DMRs for the genetic heritability of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (Fig. 4f). This enrichment was also evident in DMRs that define the transition from a healthy to an altered state (false discovery rate (FDR) = 0.004), thereby directly linking epigenetic dysregulation of this cell state to genetic risk variants for kidney disease.

### Altered proximal tubule cells show increased methylation heterogeneity and overlap with transcriptional signatures of aging
To define state-associated epigenomic changes, we compared altered- and healthy state PT cells within the same donors. This design reduces confounding from inter-individual genetic variation, chronological age and sex, allowing us to isolate the epigenetic shifts associated with the altered state (Methods). Here, the altered PT cell state designation represents a combination of the adaptive, maladaptive and degenerative states defined in the Human Kidney Atlas (HKA)9,10, grouped together to capture the epigenetic changes of tubular injury. This revealed extensive epigenetic alterations between the cell states (Fig. 5a), with a trend toward gain of methylation, as 63% of the differential methylation was hypermethylated (Supplementary Table 6). Functional annotation identified the significant enrichment of terms including ‘response to laminar fluid shear stress’ (FDR = 2 × 10−11) and ‘regulation of histone deacetylation,’ (FDR = 7.8 × 10−9) implying a significant shift of epigenetic landscape between the cell states (Supplementary Table 7). Furthermore, these methylation changes were significantly enriched within the PT-specific DMRs that defined healthy tubular identity (fold enrichment = 1.84, P < 0.01), indicating that regulatory regions of steady-state PT were specifically dysregulated in disease.
Beyond differences in mean methylation levels between healthy- and altered-state PT cell populations, altered-state PT cells showed increased local methylation heterogeneity. Single-molecule co-methylation analysis28 revealed reduced concordance among neighboring CpGs and increased methylation entropy in altered-state cells (Fig. 5b). This difference was most pronounced at PT-specific regulatory regions and is consistent with reduced local methylation coordination.
We next asked whether healthy and altered PT states differed at motifs for TFs that maintain PT identity. Matched single-cell ATAC-seq data identified motifs with greater accessibility in healthy PT cells, including motifs from the HNF, RXR and PPAR families (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The altered accessibility of RXR-associated motifs is consistent with previous findings that chromatin accessibility at these sites is dynamically regulated during AKI and that RXRα activation can mitigate subsequent fibrosis29. Altered-state PT cells showed increased methylation at these lineage-associated motifs (Supplementary Fig. 5b), suggesting that reduced activity of these regulatory programs may accompany the loss of PT identity in kidney disease.
DNA methylation differences were associated with local gene expression differences, with a negative correlation between methylation at DMRs and the expression of neighboring genes (Spearman’s rho = −0.49; Fig. 5c). We next assessed whether the transcriptional changes distinguishing altered- from healthy-state PT cells were associated with age-associated transcriptional changes, using single-nucleus data from 72 nondiseased kidneys obtained from individuals aged 20–90 years (Supplementary Fig. 6). Age-associated PT genes included CXCL1, IGFBP6 and KCNB2 (Supplementary Fig. 7), and the overall age-association scores correlated positively with altered-versus-healthy PT expression changes (Fig. 5d). This overlap indicates that altered PT cells share components of a normal aging-associated transcriptional program.
Finally, genes that were both differentially methylated and differentially expressed in the altered state were enriched for genes lacking CpG islands (CGI-less) (Fig. 5e). CGI-less genes are often located within repressive heterochromatin, including lamina-associated domains, and their misexpression has been linked to aging-associated degeneration30,31. These findings suggest that altered-state PT cells preferentially dysregulate genes embedded in repressive chromatin structures and raise the possibility that higher-order chromatin organization contributes to these expression changes.

### Disease-associated PT methylation-expression signatures localize to repair-associated spatial niches
To describe the spatial context of methylation-associated gene expression changes, we analyzed a targeted 5,000 genes with the 10x Xenium platform in four representative specimens spanning healthy, AKI and CKD tissues (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 and Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). Integration with the HKA9,10 resolved cell types spanning cortical to medullary structures, as well as adaptive or maladaptive PT states associated with resolving or unresolved repair (Supplementary Fig. 8). These cell types were further organized into niches based upon their spatial colocalizations (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 9a), to resolve normal renal structures (for example, niche 10 renal corpuscles) as well as distinct early (niche 8) or failed (niche 6) epithelial repair niches. Consistently these niches showed a switch from healthy (HNF4A) to adaptive/maladaptive (NFkB and SOX4) PT TF activities (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Furthermore, the early PT repair niche 8, enriched in the AKI sample showed increased early stress response TF activities (ATF4, JUN and BACH2) and increased activity of the E2F pathway (Fig. 6b,c and Supplementary Fig. 9b). Alternatively, the failed repair niche, enriched in CKD, showed higher TF activities associated with the failed repair PT state (BHLHE40, MYC and MITF)10 and elevated WNT signaling that has been associated with fibrosis32 (Fig. 6b,c and Supplementary Fig. 9b).
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)33 confirmed an enrichment of altered-state PT hypomethylated genes in Xenium altered PT cells, with altered-state PT hypermethylated genes enriched in the healthy state (Fig. 6d). This is consistent with trends for genes also found to be differentially expressed between these states in the 10x Multiome data (Fig. 6d). PCK1, encoding a key component of the gluconeogenesis metabolic pathway found to be critical for normal PT function, was found as a top gene that was hypermethylated and downregulated in altered-state PT cells10 (Fig. 6e). Alternatively, genes associated with cell polarity and adhesion were hypomethylated and upregulated in altered-state PT cells (PLAU, MYO1C and CYTH2), consistent with the mesenchymal-like shift associated with this state10. Spatial mapping showed differential enrichment of DMR-associated gene signatures between the healthy PT niche (niche 9) and niches enriched for early or failed repair states (early repair niche 8 and failed repair niche 6) (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 9c).

### Healthy reference PT-specific chromatin compartments are enriched for genes dysregulated in altered PT cells
To determine whether genes dysregulated in altered PT cells preferentially occupy PT-specific higher-order chromatin structure, we generated scMethyl-Hi-C profiles from a single healthy reference donor using scMethyl-Hi-C17, which simultaneously captures both 3D chromosome conformation and the DNA methylome within the same cell. We obtained 3,072 high-quality profiles and more than 300 million long-range chromosomal contacts. Integration of the methylation component with the sciMET reference atlas showed no significant batch effects between the two modalities (Fig. 7a), and chromatin-contact profiles separated major kidney cell types (Fig. 7b).
Analysis of global chromatin organization revealed cell-type-specific differences in chromatin-contact distributions. Epithelial cells displayed a higher ratio of short-range (up to 2 Mbp) to long-range (2–10 Mbp) contacts than non-epithelial cell types (Fig. 7c). Because short-range contacts contribute to local chromatin organization and gene regulation14,34, this pattern suggests that epithelial cells may rely more strongly on local 3D chromatin interactions.
To examine whether genes dysregulated in altered PT cells preferentially occupy PT-specific chromatin environments, we defined cell-type-specific A and B compartments at 250-kb resolution using the healthy reference scMethyl-Hi-C data. PT compartment scores were compared to those of other kidney cell types to identify PT-specific compartments (Methods and Fig. 7d). Genes upregulated in altered PT cells were significantly enriched within PT-specific B compartments, whereas downregulated genes were enriched within PT-specific A compartment (Fig. 7e). By integrating these compartment assignments with altered-state methylation and expression changes, we identified 107 regions consistent with putative loss of repression and 64 regions consistent with putative gain of repression (Supplementary Table 10). Most regions showed concordant methylation changes, with hypomethylation observed in 70% of putative loss-of-repression regions and hypermethylation in 78% of putative gain-of-repression regions.
Genes within putative loss-of-repression regions included inflammatory and stress response genes, such as CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3 and CXCL6, some of which overlap with previously described senescence-associated secretory phenotype profiles. These regions also contained profibrotic extracellular matrix and receptor genes, including ITGA2 and ANTXR1, as well as TFs associated with altered epithelial states, including HMGA2, ETS1 and BACH2. These findings identify PT-specific chromatin contexts in which altered methylation and gene expression coincide with inflammatory and remodeling-associated programs.
To examine the potential clinical relevance of these genes, we integrated the putative loss-of-repression gene set with UK Biobank Olink plasma-proteomic data from 54,219 participants35. Plasma levels of proteins encoded by several genes in these regions, including DSC2, DSG2 and TIMP2, were negatively associated with eGFR (Fig. 7f). Together, these analyses link altered-state methylation and expression patterns to PT-specific chromatin structures and nominate candidate genes associated with kidney function.

### Discussion
DNA methylation age acceleration has been reported across chronic diseases36,37,38,39,40, but bulk analyses obscure the responsible cell populations. Using cell-type-resolved single-cell DNA methylomes integrated with matched transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility data, spatial transcriptomics and a healthy reference 3D chromatin map, we found that disease-associated DNA methylation age acceleration was most pronounced in tubular epithelial cells. PT-specific DMRs were also enriched for eGFR heritability, linking cell-type-specific regulatory variation to kidney-function-associated loci.
Altered PT cells showed increased local methylation heterogeneity, dysregulation of lineage-associated programs, and transcriptional overlap with normal human kidney aging. Genes with coordinated methylation and expression changes were preferentially CGI-less, a class enriched for lineage-specific genes and repressive chromatin contexts31. These findings support an epigenetic and transcriptional aging-like state associated with maladaptive repair, although overlapping molecular signatures induced by injury, inflammation, stress and de-differentiation preclude definitive attribution to accelerated biological aging or cellular senescence.
Altered PT states were accompanied by reduced local methylation coordination and preferential dysregulation of genes within PT-specific chromatin compartments. Regions consistent with reduced repression contained inflammatory chemokines and TFs associated with epithelial state transitions. Plasma levels of proteins encoded by several of these genes, including DSC2, DSG2 and TIMP2, were negatively associated with eGFR in the general population35, linking these molecular features to clinically relevant kidney traits.
Epithelial cells also exhibited a higher ratio of short-range to long-range chromatin contacts, consistent with a greater contribution of local chromatin organization to epithelial gene regulation. This feature may reflect the regulatory demands of metabolically specialized tubular cells, which coordinate solute carriers, ion channels, and mitochondrial pathways to sustain renal transport functions41.
However, this dense regulatory requirement may render epithelial cells inherently more vulnerable to structural disorganization during chronic stress. Furthermore, our spatial transcriptomics further localized altered PT states to inflammatory repair niches, indicating that immune infiltration indirectly contributes to this chromatin instability. Paracrine signaling from infiltrating immune cells, such as the release of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, triggers profound intracellular stress responses in neighboring epithelia42. Prolonged exposure to this inflammatory microenvironment can alter the activity of key epigenetic modifiers8, playing a critical role in exacerbating the widespread chromatin remodeling of the maladaptive state.
The cross-species kidney epigenome atlas also revealed broad conservation of core renal cell identities but greater divergence at epithelial distal regulatory elements associated with stress responses and genome maintenance. These divergent regions may contribute to species-specific responses to aging and injury and should be considered when translating mechanistic findings across experimental models. The atlas therefore provides a resource for investigating conserved and species-specific regulatory programs in kidney aging and disease.
Our study has several limitations. First, the mouse models of aging and acute injury used in this research may not fully capture the intricate, multifaceted nature of human chronic disease. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to definitively establish a causal link between epigenetic aging and kidney disease progression. Second, our cross-species comparisons primarily focused on orthologous bins and mapped DMRs. Consequently, species-specific sequences and human genetic variations might introduce additional effects that were not entirely accounted for in this study. Furthermore, our mouse cohort consisted exclusively of male animals. Given the well-documented sex-dependent differences in PT physiology, metabolism and gene regulation, it is possible that observed species-specific epigenetic divergence and age-related changes are influenced by sex biases43,44,45. Future studies incorporating both male and female cohorts across the lifespan will be necessary to fully disentangle conserved epigenetic mechanisms from sex-specific regulatory networks. Additionally, the small sample size and heterogeneity of our human disease cohort limited our ability to perform etiology-stratified analyses. Consequently, our findings likely reflect a generalized epigenetic response to chronic kidney injury, and future studies with larger, etiology-matched cohorts will be necessary to isolate disease-specific epigenetic nuances. Last, while our scMethyl-Hi-C data provided a structural scaffold for healthy kidney cell types, relying on a single reference donor limits our ability to capture inter-individual 3D structural variation. Due to the relatively sparse resolution of scMethyl-Hi-C, we likely underestimated the full extent of chromatin loop disruption. Despite these limitations, our study collectively provides valuable insights into the cell-type-level epigenomes in the kidney, providing a foundational resource for future investigations and deciphering genome-wide methylation-based physiological decline of kidney function.
