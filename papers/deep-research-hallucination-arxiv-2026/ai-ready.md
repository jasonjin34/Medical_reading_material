# Why Your Deep Research Agent Fails? On Hallucination Evaluation in Full Research Trajectory

- **Authors:** Yuhao Zhan; Tianyu Fan; Linxuan Huang; Zirui Guo; Chao Huang

## Abstract
Diagnosing failure patterns in Deep Research Agents (DRAs) remains a critical challenge. Existing benchmarks predominantly rely on end-to-end evaluation, obscuring intermediate hallucinations that accumulate throughout the research trajectory. To bridge this gap, we propose a shift from outcome-based to process-aware evaluation by auditing hallucinations in the full plan-search-summarize trajectory. We introduce the PING Taxonomy, which categorizes DRA hallucinations into four complementary types: Propagation, Intent, Noise-induced, and Grounding. We further instantiate this taxonomy into a fine-grained evaluation framework that decomposes trajectories into atomic actions, claims, and sub-queries for rigorous verification, and we validate its reliability on standard fact-checking benchmarks and human-reviewed trajectories. Leveraging this framework to isolate 100 hallucination-prone tasks, including adversarial scenarios, we curate DeepHalluBench. Experiments on six representative DRAs show that, on our hallucination-prone stress-test set, all evaluated systems still exhibit non-negligible reliability gaps. Furthermore, our diagnostic analysis traces these failures to systemic deficits, especially hallucination propagation and cognitive biases, providing actionable insights for future architectural optimization. Code and data are available at https://github.com/yuhao-zhan/DeepHalluBench.

1 Introduction
The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has spurred the development of Deep Research Agents (DRAs) Huang et al. (2025); Zhang et al. (2025b). A DRA iteratively plans, searches, and reasons over external information to produce a final report for a user query. Systems such as OpenAI’s OpenAI (2025) and Gemini’s Gemini (2025) can reduce tasks that previously took substantial manual effort to minutes.
Figure 1: Comparison of DRA benchmarks: Outcome-oriented vs. process-aware hallucination auditing.
Despite their potential, DRAs remain difficult to evaluate from final outputs alone. Existing benchmarks largely fall into two categories: close-ended benchmarks that verify short-form answers against groundtruth Mialon et al. (2023); Wei et al. (2025), and open-ended benchmarks that assess long-form reports with reference reports or rubrics Li et al. (2026); Li et al. (2025a); Wang et al. (2026b). Most are outcome-oriented, treating the research process as a black box (Figure 1). Although some recent work is process-aware Ye et al. (2026); Chen et al. (2026), it does not specifically target DRA hallucinations.
This leads to two major limitations. (1) Incomplete Hallucination Detection. Critical intermediate hallucinations, such as misleading plans, are only partially captured because current evaluations do not systematically audit hallucinations across the full trajectory. (2) Opaque Performance Diagnosis. Without a comprehensive trajectory-level audit, attributing failures to specific stages (e.g., planning or summarization) remains difficult, impeding fine-grained understanding and optimization. Addressing these challenges requires a shift from outcome-based assessment to process-aware evaluation, capable of auditing hallucinations throughout the research trajectory.
However, realizing such process-aware evaluation faces three obstacles. (1) Taxonomic Gap. Hallucination taxonomies tailored to DRAs remain under-explored. (2) Data Acquisition Barriers. Proprietary DRAs either impose prohibitive API costs or operate via Web UIs lacking structured logs (e.g., JSON), complicating automated tracking. (3) Evaluation Complexity. To overcome these barriers, we first address the taxonomic gap. We model the research trajectory as iterative plan-search-summarize loops,11
 1
 The final report is treated as the terminal summary. where planning produces actions, search is mediated by an external engine, and summarization produces claims. We then introduce the PING Taxonomy, which organizes hallucination-related failures observed in DRA trajectories into four categories: Grounding (fabricated or misattributed claims against retrieved sources), Noise-induced (failure to prioritize the most informative retrieved evidence), Intent (planning-stage failures that misinterpret or incompletely satisfy the user query), and Propagation (later actions built on earlier hallucinated content).
Building on this taxonomy, we address the remaining two obstacles. For Data Acquisition Barriers, since proprietary DRAs generally lack cost-effective APIs and structured logs, we develop parsers that reconstruct unstructured Web UI traces into standardized plan-search-summarize loops, and we manually verify the parsed output against the rendered HTML. For Evaluation Complexity, we decompose trajectories into atomic actions, claims, and sub-queries, enabling fine-grained verification of source faithfulness, context utilization, intent satisfaction, and error propagation. To ensure the pipeline’s reliability, we validate it on standard fact-checking benchmarks and human-reviewed trajectories.
Leveraging this framework, we introduce DeepHalluBench, the first benchmark designed to evaluate hallucinations across observable DRA research trajectories. We construct it by aggregating diverse queries from existing benchmarks Gou et al. (2025); Fan et al. (2025); Wei et al. (2025), synthesizing adversarial “no-answer” queries via atomic perturbations, and filtering for the most hallucination-prone queries under our evaluation pipeline, yielding 100 queries balanced between open- and close-ended tasks.
Using DeepHalluBench, we benchmark five proprietary and one open-source DRA to study two questions. For RQ1 (Hallucination Landscape), we find that all evaluated agents show measurable weaknesses on at least one dimension, including grounding, intent satisfaction, or information prioritization. For RQ2 (Failure Mechanisms), we trace these failures to systemic causes, especially hallucination propagation and cognitive biases such as the temporal “Anchor Effect” and semantic “Homogeneity Bias”. These findings suggest that future progress requires more than retrieval scaling, instead calling for architectures that support early error correction and more reliable long-context attention.
Contributions. (1) We introduce the PING Taxonomy for systematically characterizing DRA hallucinations across the full research trajectory. (2) We develop a corresponding evaluation framework and construct DeepHalluBench, the first benchmark dedicated to trajectory-level stress-testing of DRA hallucinations. (3) We provide a diagnostic analysis of six DRAs, identifying key failure mechanisms such as hallucination propagation and cognitive biases.
2 Related Work
We discuss only the most relevant studies and provide
further details in Appendix A.
Hallucinations.
LLM hallucinations are generally defined as content that is nonsensical or unfaithful to source materials Farquhar et al. (2024), typically categorized as input-, context-, or fact-conflicting Zhang et al. (2025c). Beyond these, intent hallucination Hao et al. (2025) encompasses the omission or misinterpretation of instructions. In agentic workflows, this scope expands to fact omission, where LLMs fail to present contextually supported claims Ma et al. (2024), necessitating process-aware taxonomies Zhu et al. (2025); Kim et al. (2025); Liu et al. (2026). Hallucination detection widely employs claim verification Zerong et al. (2025) using Natural Language Inference (NLI) Chen et al. (2025b); Schopf et al. (2025), LLMs Wei et al. (2024); Rahman et al. (2026), or agents Cheng et al. (2024). However, hallucinations specific to DRAs lack systematic taxonomy and detection Gou et al. (2025), leaving their fundamental limitations largely unexplored.
Deep Research Evaluation.
Current DRA benchmarks evaluate close-ended tasks via accuracy Mialon et al. (2023); Wei et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2025a) or open-ended reports via rubrics Gou et al. (2025); Li et al. (2026); Fan et al. (2025); Li et al. (2025a); Xie et al. (2026), expanding to automated arena-based ranking Gao et al. (2026) and robustness to misleading documents Zhu et al. (2026). While recent benchmarks Ye et al. (2026); Chen et al. (2026); Sabharwal et al. (2026) introduce process-aware metrics, most evaluations remain outcome-oriented and are not specifically tailored to hallucinations. This motivates a complementary framework that explicitly links final failures to intermediate claims, actions, and retrieved evidence (See Table 1).

3 Hallucination Taxonomy
Traditional LLM hallucination taxonomies do not fully capture the multi-faceted, search-based, and long-horizon nature of DRAs. A DRA operates through iterative plan-search-summarize loops: planning produces actions (including search-query formulation), search is mediated by an external engine, and summarization produces claims. 22
 2
 We treat the search engine as an external information source and focus on LLM-induced hallucinations at the plan and summarize stages. To this end, we propose the PING Taxonomy (Propagation, Intent, Noise-induced, Grounding), which organizes hallucinations by their locus of failure: source-level faithfulness, query-level alignment, context-level utilization, and trajectory-level error propagation. This organization extends prior notions of intent hallucination Hao et al. (2025) and fact omission in RAG Ma et al. (2024) to the long-horizon, search-based DRA setting. As illustrated in Figure 2, this yields four categories of DRA hallucinations:
Grounding Hallucination (Source-Level):
Occurs during summarization when the DRA generates claims unfaithful to the retrieved source materials. It includes:
(1) Fabrication: Generating claims unsupported by the evidence available in the recorded trajectory.
(2) Misattribution: Citing documents in the recorded evidence scope that do not support the claim, even when other retrieved but uncited sources may support it.
Noise-induced Hallucination (Context-Level):
Refers to a context-utilization failure in which query-relevant evidence has been retrieved but underused, increasing the risk of unsupported or incomplete conclusions. As with fact omission in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) Ma et al. (2024), the correct information is available in context but critically neglected.
Intent Hallucination (Query-Level):
Even when individual claims are supported by retrieved evidence, the DRA may still fail to satisfy the user’s intent Hao et al. (2025). This category covers planning-stage failures, including flawed search-query formulation and task decomposition. It manifests in two forms:
(1) Restriction Neglect: The DRA formulates a plan that is technically executable but silently ignores specific user restrictions (e.g., ignoring “full-time” in a job-seeking task, Figure 2).
(2) Deviated Actions: The DRA’s action sequence, including search-query formulation and reformulation, actively deviates from or misinterprets the user intent.
Propagation Hallucination (Trajectory-Level):
Occurs when later actions are built on earlier hallucinated content. This cascading failure is specific to the multi-round nature of DRAs, where a single grounding or intent error can propagate and amplify throughout the research process.
4 Evaluation and Benchmark
Guided by the PING taxonomy, this section describes our framework for trajectory data acquisition and fine-grained hallucination evaluation.
4.1 Data Acquisition and Decomposition
To evaluate proprietary DRAs lacking cost-friendly APIs and structured reasoning output, our pipeline reconstructs observable research traces from Web UI outputs. We employ custom HTML-parsers and LLMs to disentangle interleaved reasoning and URLs into structured plan-search-summarize loops. We verify the parsed output against the rendered HTML and treat the parsed trajectories as observable proxies of the provider-exposed process. To quantify hallucinations precisely, we adopt an atomicity-based approach Min et al. (2023); Yan et al. (2025), decomposing the trajectory into atomic units: user query into atomic sub-queries, plans into atomic actions, and summaries into atomic claims (preserving citation mappings). A two-stage decompose-then-refine LLM pipeline ensures the atomicity and verifiability of these extracted units (Appendix B.4). All prompt templates in this work are in Appendix F.

Claim Verification for Grounding Hallucinations.
To distinguish between factual observations and internal reflections, we implement a two-round verification pipeline (Figure 3).
• 
Round 1: Initial Verification. We verify claims against their evidence scope, cited documents for report claims and the full retrieval history for others, via a retrieve-then-verify strategy with a cost-efficient NLI-then-LLM cascade. Supported claims and their evidence chunks are stored in Claim Memory and Chunk Memory, respectively.
• 
Round 2: Adaptive Re-Verification. Unsupported claims trigger branching checks to categorize errors:
(1) Misattribution Check: For claims with explicit citations, we expand the evidence scope to all retrieved documents. Support here indicates misattribution $C_{\mathrm{misattribution}}$ . Otherwise, the claim is confirmed as fabrication $C_{\mathrm{fabrication}}$ .
(2) Reflection Check: For intermediate claims, we compare them against Claim Memory to assess whether they are consistent with previously supported trajectory content. Lack of support confirms fabrication $C_{\mathrm{fabrication}}$ .

Reliability.
We validate the pipeline through standard fact-checking benchmarks, mainstream configuration checks, and a qualitative human audit. The claim-verification module reaches 95.2% accuracy on FEVER Thorne et al. (2018) and 87.3% on SciFact-Open Wadden et al. (2022). Validations and ablations of the pipeline’s internal settings, including the retrieval similarity threshold, chunk length, NLI confidence threshold, and top-K evidence selection, are detailed in Appendix B.2. For components without objective benchmarks, we additionally audited 20 trajectories outside the final benchmark queries. Two annotators with NLP and fact-checking backgrounds independently annotated the same 20 trajectories, about 8,800 atomic units in total, with a raw inter-annotator agreement of $\kappa=0.83$ . Disagreements were resolved through discussion, bringing the agreement to 1.0, and the adjudicated labels guided three rounds of evaluator refinement, after which the evaluator reached the clean level (Level 3) of the audit scale. See Appendix B.4 for the audit protocol.

5 Results & Analysis
This section investigates two core research questions (RQs): RQ1 (What): What characterizes the hallucination landscape of current DRAs? RQ2 (Why): What trajectory-level patterns are associated with DRA failures? We first benchmark representative DRAs on DeepHalluBench, followed by a diagnostic analysis of recurring failure patterns.

5.3 Analysis
Based on this multifaceted hallucination landscape, we delve into RQ2 (Why): What trajectory-level patterns are associated with DRA failures? We identify two primary failure etiologies: hallucination propagation and cognitive biases.
Hallucination Propagation.
Hallucinations in DRAs exhibit strong sequential dependency (“domino effect”). We map these into a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), where an edge $A\rightarrow B$ models a potential dependency in which a later error $B$ is supported by or derived from an earlier hallucination $A$ . We identify these links by detecting NLI-based entailment between claims and identifying actions built on hallucinated premises (the propagation set $A_{\mathrm{propagation}}$ , see Appendix E.1). Figure 6 reveals distinct temporal profiles:
• 
Early-Stage Cascading (Gemini, OpenAI). These systems show systemic cascading, with $>57\%$ of source errors occurring in the early stage. Initial fabrication often appears near the beginning of the dependency chains, suggesting that early unsupported claims make later steps more vulnerable.
• 
Late-Stage Collapse (Salesforce). In contrast, the open-source framework shows fewer early dependency-chain sources but a larger share of late-stage errors ( $>40\%$ of errors). This highlights a limitation in maintaining coherence over long contexts.

Root-Cause Patterns in Close-Ended Failures.
We further analyze root-cause errors (the earliest step precipitating final failure) for the 50 close-ended queries (Figure 13 in Appendix E.2). Two mechanisms emerge: (1) Fabrication as a Frequent Precursor: for most DRAs, intermediate summarization fabrication frequently precedes incorrect close-ended outcomes, with conclusions unsupported by retrieved documents. (2) Divergent Fallbacks: in trajectories free of hallucinations, the open-source DRA (i.e., Salesforce) conservatively refuses the query, whereas proprietary DRAs often fabricate a final answer. This aligns with the “Over-Confidence vs. Over-Conservatism” dichotomy in Table 3.
Cognitive Biases.
Our measurements suggest that DRAs do not use retrieved information uniformly across temporal and semantic dimensions, a pattern that contributes to noise-induced context underuse.
• 
Temporal: “Anchor Effect”. Figure 5 shows that DRAs disproportionately favor early retrieval. The Utilized Chunk Count drops precipitously in later stages. Interestingly, Noise Scores tend to increase in later stages even when our Retrieval Quality66
 6

This indicates a “saturation” bottleneck: agents may stop attending to new, superior information once their context is filled by initial findings, which can also be viewed as premature convergence on early evidence.
• 
Semantic: “Homogeneity Bias”. DRAs prefer redundancy over novelty. In several systems, higher information heterogeneity correlates with increased neglect, suggesting that DRAs are overwhelmed as the retrieval context becomes more diverse (Figure 7). Furthermore, we compare small and large clusters within the same relevance range (i.e., with equal value) and find that smaller clusters are ignored more often in every DRA (Table 4). DRAs prefer homogeneous, frequently repeated evidence over heterogeneous but less frequent information, even when their relevance is comparable, which we call Homogeneity Bias.
In summary, while hallucinations originate from the backbone LLM, our measurements localize them to the planning, summarization, and trajectory stages, revealing failure patterns that are invisible from final answers alone. The backbone ablation (Appendix D.2) further suggests these patterns persist after a stronger backbone is used. Reliability therefore requires architectural interventions, such as early-stage error correction and attention debiasing, rather than simple retrieval scaling.
6 Conclusion
Deep Research Agents are increasingly deployed for open-ended, long-horizon research tasks, yet existing evaluations largely judge only their final reports, leaving intermediate hallucinations unaccounted for. We address this gap with a process-aware framework: the PING Taxonomy organizes hallucinations by their locus of failure in the research trajectory, our evaluation framework instantiates this taxonomy into fine-grained verification over atomic actions, claims, and sub-queries, and DeepHalluBench curates a stress-test set that reliably exposes them. On this stress-test set, all six evaluated DRAs still exhibit substantial weaknesses in grounding, intent satisfaction, context utilization, and long-horizon error control. We trace these failures to systemic mechanisms, especially hallucination propagation and cognitive biases such as the Anchor Effect and Homogeneity Bias, rather than isolated model errors. Improving DRA reliability therefore requires not only stronger base models, but also better architectures for intermediate verification, memory use, and error correction across the research trajectory.
Limitations
This work has several limitations. First, our framework diagnoses where hallucinations arise in the agentic workflow, but not the intrinsic parametric causes inside the backbone LLM. It therefore complements, rather than replaces, mechanistic analyses of model behavior. Second, our current taxonomy is primarily centered on textual trajectories. Extending these process-aware metrics to multimodal web content (e.g., images, video) represents a natural next step for the community as these agents evolve. Third, our atomicity-based evaluation prioritizes diagnostic depth over scale, making it more expensive than lightweight end-to-end metrics. Future work may develop distilled evaluators for scalable process-aware auditing, extend the framework to multimodal web content, and extend the open-source comparison as resources permit.
